## Intuition

- **Mean** centers; **variance/std** spreads. Financial returns are often heavy-tailed, so “mean ± 1 std” is not the whole story.
- **Quantiles** (5%/95%) speak about tails without assuming normality.
- **Correlation** summarizes linear co-movement; short samples are noisy; rolling correlation drifts.
- **IID often fails** in markets: volatility clusters; regimes break. Textbook p-value formulas that assume IID then **overstate** significance (a theme AFML stresses for financial data).
- **Many trials**: among 100 random rules, a few look great in-sample — that is multiple comparison, not discovery. Shipping the best in-sample curve is **backtest overfitting** (AFML spirit).

## Formulas

Sample variance with (n-1) degrees of freedom:

<Formula label="Unbiased sample variance">
s^2 = sum_i (x_i - mean)^2 / (n - 1)
</Formula>

Std s = sqrt(s^2).  
A teaching Sharpe (zero rf) on daily returns:

<Formula label="Annualized Sharpe sketch">
Sharpe ≈ mean(r) / std(r) * sqrt(252)
</Formula>

When n=252, the t-statistic mean/(std/sqrt(n)) for “mean = 0” matches that Sharpe sketch numerically — a teaching coincidence for comparison, not a definition identity.

## Why read quantiles together with volatility

Means get yanked by a few huge days; variance/std describe dispersion; quantiles answer “how bad is a 5% day?” Honest research reports all three, not a lone Sharpe.

## Worked numeric example (hand calculation)

Returns 0.01, -0.02, 0.015:

- Mean \bar r ≈ 0.001667
- Sum of squared deviations over 2 → sample variance **var≈0.000358**, std **std≈0.0189**

## P-value misuse: numeric walk-through

Parameters (same as the lab): **n=252** days, annualization **√252**, threshold **|SR|>2**, demo seed **7**, **100** random-sign rules per run.

Under the null, if daily active returns are roughly IID and SR_ann = mean/std×√252 has sd ≈ 1, then **P(|Z|>2)≈4.55%**, i.e. about **4–5** hits per 100 trials. Seed **7** is an extreme draw that produced **10** (see the multi-seed sweep below: across 200 seeds, mean ≈ **4.40**, std ≈ **2.15**, and seed 7 hit the sweep maximum of 10).

Null H0: mean daily PnL of a random-sign rule is 0.  
Among those **100** trials, pick the largest |Sharpe|, then compute a two-sided normal-approx p-value — here about **p≈0.0013** with |Sharpe|≈3.22.

Reporting **only that trial** as “p under 0.01, therefore significant” misuses the p-value: the significance budget never paid for searching 100 rules. That is multiple testing / backtest overfitting (AFML). Honest practice at least pre-registers hypotheses, reports trial counts, and uses out-of-sample or combinatorial penalties.

## Complete multiple-testing loop (shown in the lesson)

Fixed: `n=252`, `Sharpe = mean/std*sqrt(252)`, threshold `|Sharpe|>2`, demo seed `7` (see also the 200-seed sweep in the script).


```python
rng_mt = np.random.default_rng(7)
false_hits = 0
sharpes = []
for i in range(100):
    noise = rng_mt.normal(size=n)
    pos = np.sign(noise)
    pnl = pos * r_a.values
    sh = sharpe(pd.Series(pnl))  # mean/std*sqrt(252)
    sharpes.append(sh)
    if abs(sh) > 2:
        false_hits += 1
print("trials", 100)
print("false_hits_|Sharpe|>2", false_hits)
print("max_abs_sharpe", round(max(abs(s) for s in sharpes), 3))
```

## Common mistakes / pitfalls

1. Treating one lucky in-sample Sharpe (or tiny p) as a repeatable edge.
2. Assuming IID normality despite clustered volatility (same warning spirit as AFML).
3. Confusing variance with std (different units).
4. Estimating correlation on tiny windows without uncertainty.
5. Reading correlation as causation.
6. Searching thousands of parameters and posting only the best curve — classic backtest overfitting.
