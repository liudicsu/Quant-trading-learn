## Kelly and fractional Kelly

Kelly (1956, *Bell System Technical Journal*) maximises expected log wealth. For an even-money bet with win probability `p`, `f* = 2p − 1`. Thorp (handbook chapter, e.g. 2006) emphasises **fractional Kelly**: over-betting destroys growth; half-Kelly trades some growth for materially smaller drawdowns.

Lab (`examples/p6/01_portfolio_risk_sizing.py`):

| Policy | f | median log growth | ruin rate (&lt;1% wealth) | median max DD |
|--------|---|-------------------|--------------------------|---------------|
| full Kelly | 0.10 | ≈ +2.50 | 0.00 | ≈ −0.80 |
| **half Kelly** | 0.05 | ≈ +1.88 | 0.00 | ≈ **−0.53** |
| 2× Kelly | 0.20 | ≈ **−0.07** | ≈ **0.16** | ≈ −1.00 |

**Ruin definition (lab):** a path counts as ruined if **terminal wealth &lt; 1% of starting wealth**.

**Expected-value context:** full Kelly maximises *median asymptotic growth* under a known edge — not comfort, not VaR, not institutional mandate. With a **noisy estimated** edge, full Kelly is an unsafe upper bound.

## Volatility targeting

Scale exposure so that `w_t · σ̂_{t−1} ≈ σ_target`. Lab: raw ann. vol ≈ 0.27 → vol-targeted ≈ 0.11. Look-ahead rule: estimate vol on past returns only; apply weight on the **next** bar.

## Risk parity: inverse-vol and ERC

- **Inverse-vol:** `w_i ∝ 1/σ_i` (ignores correlations).
- **ERC (equal risk contribution):** choose `w` so each asset’s `w_i · ∂σ_p/∂w_i` is equal (Maillard–Roncalli–Teiletche style). Lab iterative ERC drives last-day RC std ≈ 0.

Lab OOS Sharpe (synthetic, negative-drift foil): inv-vol ≈ −0.21, ERC ≈ −0.12, equal-weight ≈ −0.31. Point is the **risk budget**, not a promised SR.

## Mean-variance, estimation error, 1/N, Ledoit–Wolf

Markowitz (1952, *Journal of Finance*) gives the classic MV programme. Michaud (1989) warns that optimisers are **error maximisers** — they overweight the noisiest μ/Σ entries. DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal (2009, *RFS* 22(5)) show many optimised rules fail to beat **1/N** out of sample.

Ledoit & Wolf publish two 2004 papers used here:

1. **JPM 30(4)** — “Honey, I Shrunk the Sample Covariance Matrix” (constant-correlation target; portfolio IR motivation).
2. **JMVA 88(2)** — well-conditioned large-dimensional shrinkage (the family behind sklearn `LedoitWolf`).

Lab walk-forward OOS Sharpe: sample MV ≈ −0.215, LedoitWolf MV ≈ −0.211, 1/N ≈ −0.461; mean shrinkage intensity ≈ **0.036**. On *this* panel MV beats 1/N — DeMiguel’s result is “often,” not “always.” Shrinkage still improves conditioning of Σ.

![MV vs 1/N](/p6/mv_vs_1n.png)

## Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP) vs ERC

The 2016 paper title contains “Outperform Out of Sample” — that is a **historical paper title**, not a promised OOS Sharpe upgrade for your book. HRP is a **hierarchy allocation method that avoids inverting Σ**.

**Intuition:** Markowitz-style optimizers invert a covariance matrix and can **concentrate** when assets are highly correlated (“error maximization,” Michaud). **Hierarchical Risk Parity** (López de Prado, *Journal of Portfolio Management* 2016; AFML ch.16) never inverts \(\Sigma\). It (1) turns correlations into distances and builds a dendrogram, (2) quasi-diagonalizes by leaf order, (3) recursively bisects and allocates with inverse-variance cluster variances.

**Same 8-asset toy** (4 equity / 2 bond / 2 commodity factors):

| | Equity cluster sum | Bond | Commodity |
|--|-------------------|------|-----------|
| HRP | ≈**0.42** | ≈0.27 | ≈0.31 |
| ERC (opt.) | ≈**0.50** | ≈0.25 | ≈0.25 |
| inv-vol | ≈0.53 | ≈0.23 | ≈0.25 |

HRP trims the large correlated equity block relative to ticker-count / inv-vol thinking. ERC equalizes risk contributions (RC std≈0 here); inv-vol ignores correlation clusters.

## No-trade region / cost-in-CV spirit

With proportional costs, optimal rebalancing is usually **not** “always return to target.” [Brandt, Santa-Clara & Valkanov (RFS 2009)](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/22/9/3411/1572695) implement a practical **no-trade** boundary (hypersphere around target weights): if holdings are inside, **do nothing**; if outside, trade toward the boundary — not necessarily all the way to target. Classic continuous-time roots: Magill–Constantinides / Davis–Norman style no-trade regions.

Lab toy:

```text
rmse(hold,target)=0.0294  k=0.0500  inside_no_trade=True
turnover_if_full_rebalance=0.0800  turnover_with_no_trade_policy=0.0000
```

**Bridge to Detzel (JF 2023):** costs change which factors/models win — so choose the no-trade radius / turnover penalty **inside** validation (cost-in-CV), not after peeking at OOS.



## Alpha decay + costs → no-trade band

**Intuition:** When predictive signal **decays** and trading is costly, the optimum is often **not** to chase every tick of alpha. [Ma & Smith (arXiv:2502.04284)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04284) study multi-period optimal trading with **alpha decay + transaction costs** — deepening the Brandt–Santa-Clara–Valkanov **no-trade** spirit: **不交易也是决策** (not trading is also a decision).

Optional NAME-ONLY foil: [arXiv:2411.07949](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07949) (prior two-parameter note).

**Toy (lab):** signal `Y_t = 0.8*X_t + 0.4*X_(t-1) + eps`; widen the half-band as cost rises:

Choose band width **inside** validation (cost-in-CV) — not after peeking at OOS. Thin pointer in `execution-slippage` (TCA / participation caps sit next to this logic).

## Mini-lab: weight drift & constraint consistency

**Intuition:** your target weights hold only at the moment you rebalance. Afterwards assets move differently and the actual weights “drift” on their own; and the constraints themselves may contradict each other (no solution at all).

**Formula:** without trading, drifted weight `w_i' = w_i · g_i / Σ_j (w_j · g_j)` (`g_i` = cumulative growth factor over the period); one-way turnover back to target `= ½ · Σ_i |w_i − w_i'|`. The simplest consistency check: with per-asset cap and n assets, if `n × cap < 1` the weights can never sum to 1.

**Numeric example (`examples/p6/05_weight_drift.py`, seed=3, synthetic, 4 assets, target 30/30/20/20%, cap 33%):**

```text
cap=0.33, n=4: max total = 1.32 -> feasible
cap=0.20, n=4: max total = 0.80 -> INFEASIBLE (cannot sum to 1)
A: target=0.3000 drifted=0.3462
sum drifted = 1.0000; cap breached: ['A']
one-way turnover = 0.0476; cost @ 10bp = 0.000048 of NAV
assets outside +/-2% band: ['A', 'C']
```

How to read it: the target satisfies the 33% cap, but after 63 days without trading A has drifted to 34.6% — **the cap is already breached**. So check constraints on the **drifted actual weights**, not only on the target you set. With cap=20% the constraints have no solution and should raise an error before any optimisation.

## Margin accounts: borrow at most half, keep equity at or above 25% (checked 2026-11-10)

**Leverage in a US stock account has rules.** Under the Federal Reserve's [Regulation T](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-220.12) (Reg T), initial margin on a marginable stock is 50% of its value, so you can borrow at most half. After the purchase, [FINRA Rule 4210](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/4210) (FINRA, the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, is the self-regulator for US brokers) requires your equity (position value minus the loan) to stay at or above 25% of the long market value. That is maintenance margin. A margin account also needs at least $2,000 of equity, or the full cost if the purchase is smaller. Brokers may set stricter "house" levels; the SEC says 30%–40% is typical ([SEC investor bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins-29)). The bulletin's example: buy $16,000 of stock with $8,000 of your own money and $8,000 borrowed. If the stock falls to $12,000, equity is $4,000. A 25% requirement needs $3,000, so you are fine. A 40% requirement needs $4,800, so you are $800 short and get a margin call. The broker may also sell your shares without notice. For a long-only position with no new deposits, the trigger price is: loan ÷ (shares × (1 − maintenance ratio)). FINRA's 2026 intraday margin rule (see `compliance-ethics`) "does not change the regular maintenance margin requirements"; it adds a layer on top. When volatility targeting above asks for a weight above 100%, that is borrowed money: a leveraged backtest must simulate margin calls, forced liquidation and financing interest.

**Lab:** `examples/p6/11_margin_call_toy.py` (long-only toy, no interest):

```text
=== A. Reg T initial margin ===
buy $16,000 of stock -> may borrow at most $8,000
=== B. SEC bulletin example: value falls to $12,000, loan $8,000 ===
maintenance 25%: equity $4,000 vs required $3,000 -> ok
maintenance 40%: equity $4,000 vs required $4,800 -> margin call $800
=== C. trigger price (160 shares bought at $100, loan $8,000) ===
maintenance 25%: call when price < 66.6667
maintenance 40%: call when price < 83.3333
price 75.00: below 40% trigger? True; below 25% trigger? False
assertions passed: 6/6
ASSERT_margin_call_ok
```

### Portfolio margin: margin set by stress scenarios (checked 2026-11-10)

The 50%/25% figures above are "strategy-based" margin. [FINRA Rule 4210(g)](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/4210) also lets brokers use portfolio margin for eligible accounts. A stock and its options are grouped together and revalued with an SEC-approved pricing model under a range of up and down price moves; the largest theoretical loss becomes the requirement. For a single stock the range is plus or minus 15%.

The toy below simplifies by valuing options at expiry intrinsic value only. Long 100 shares at 100 needs $5,000 of Reg T initial margin and $2,500 of FINRA maintenance margin, while the largest loss across the ±15% scenarios is $1,500. Adding one 95-strike put bought for 2 cuts the largest loss to $700. Hedges count, but even unhedged positions can need less, so the same money can carry more leverage and more risk. These accounts are only for customers approved for uncovered option writing, are not available for IRAs, and require a special risk disclosure. In research, state which margin regime you assume.

**Lab:** new section D of `examples/p6/11_margin_call_toy.py` (sections A–C above are unchanged). The rule says "ten equidistant intervals (valuation points)"; the toy reads that as 10 evenly spaced points from −15% to +15% including both ends, which is one reading of the text. Real calculations use an SEC-approved theoretical pricing model, not intrinsic value:

```text
=== D. portfolio margin (toy) ===
valuation points (%): [-15.0, -11.67, -8.33, -5.0, -1.67, 1.67, 5.0, 8.33, 11.67, 15.0]
stock only (100 sh @ 100): Reg T initial 5000.00, FINRA maint 2500.00, PM max loss 1500.00
stock + 95 put (intrinsic-only toy, premium 2.00): PM max loss 700.00 (> per-contract minimum 37.50)
assertions passed: 4/4
ASSERT_portfolio_margin_ok
disclaimer: intrinsic-value toy, not a broker's PM model; eligibility rules apply; learning only
```

## Common mistakes / pitfalls

- Ignoring alpha decay and always full-rebalancing to target under costs.
- Setting the no-trade band after peeking at OOS (must be cost-in-CV).

1. Full Kelly on a noisy estimated edge.
2. Using same-bar vol for today’s weight (look-ahead).
3. Calling inverse-vol “ERC” when correlations are ignored.
4. In-sample MV charts as proof of OOS skill.
5. Treating 1/N as universally optimal rather than a tough benchmark (DeMiguel et al.).
6. Inverting a singular/ill-conditioned Σ when HRP would have avoided the inverse.
7. Ignoring dendrogram clusters and treating every ticker as an independent risk bet.
8. Checking constraints only on target weights, ignoring drifted actual weights; optimising before checking the constraints are feasible (e.g. n×cap &lt; 1).
9. Running a leveraged backtest (vol-target weight above 100%) without margin-call checks, forced selling or financing interest; assuming 25% is your broker's level when house levels are often 30%–40%.
10. Assuming every account uses 50%/25%, or treating portfolio margin as free leverage; backtesting with a lower requirement without saying which margin regime and eligibility you assume.
