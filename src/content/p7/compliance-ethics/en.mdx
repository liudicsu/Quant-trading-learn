### Red lines before you write a trading algorithm: wash trades, self-trades, disruptive quoting, CAT (checked 2026-11-10)

General information, not legal advice. First, separate two words that are easy to mix up. The wash sale in the tax box below is a tax concept. A **wash trade** is market manipulation: a trade that involves no change in beneficial ownership, done to create a false appearance of active trading. Section 9(a) of the Securities Exchange Act prohibits such trades and prearranged matched orders. It also prohibits a series of transactions that creates actual or apparent active trading, or moves the price, in order to induce others to buy or sell ([15 U.S.C. 78i](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2023-title15/html/USCODE-2023-title15-chap2B-sec78i.htm), govinfo 2023 edition; section 9(a) matches the [current text](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-138/t15/s78i/) through Pub. L. 119-138).

Two rules matter most for coders. One is self-trades, where a firm's own buy and sell orders meet. Supplementary Material .02 to [FINRA Rule 5210](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/5210) says unintentional self-trades are generally bona fide. But firms must have procedures to review for, and prevent, a pattern of self-trades from a single algorithm or related algorithms. In code, that means self-trade prevention at the order layer, plus logs. The other is Supplementary Material .03 to the same rule, which describes two prohibited patterns of "disruptive quoting and trading". In Type 1, a party places several limit orders at different prices on one side, the apparent supply and demand shifts, the party trades on the other side, and then cancels the original orders. In Type 2, a party narrows the spread with an order inside the NBBO, then trades on the other side against participants who joined the new inside price. The rule targets a frequent pattern, including one spread across several venues. FINRA 5210 SM .03(c): the order of events does not change applicability; the rule targets frequently occurring patterns.

Finally, assume your order history can be traced: the Consolidated Audit Trail (CAT) is the consolidated audit trail the SEC required under Rule 613 (adopted 2012-07-11). On 2026-04-16 the SEC began a comprehensive review of the CAT and asked for public comment ([SEC press release 2026-37](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2026-37-sec-seeks-public-comment-consolidated-audit-trail-other-audit-trails-data-sources)). The comment period closed on 2026-06-22 (File S7-2026-12, Release 34-105251, [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/04/20/2026-07651/concept-release-on-consolidated-audit-trail-and-other-audit-trails-and-data-sources)); that review has not concluded.

**W-8BEN and dividend withholding:** the [IRS instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/iw8ben): a foreign individual gives Form W-8BEN to the **withholding agent/payer**, not to the IRS; foreign persons are generally subject to US tax at 30% on certain US-source income (including dividends), which a treaty may reduce. The [US–China treaty](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-trty/china.pdf) Article 9: when the recipient is a resident of the other state and the beneficial owner, source-state tax on dividends shall not exceed 10% of the gross amount (see also the [IRS treaty page](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/claiming-tax-treaty-benefits)). **Chinese nationality ≠ Chinese tax residence ≠ automatic treaty eligibility**; withholding is not the final settlement of all tax.

**Wash sale (per review item R25):** where the relevant US income-tax rules apply, acquiring substantially identical stock or securities by purchase or taxable exchange, or entering into a contract or option to acquire them, within the period from 30 calendar days before to 30 calendar days after the loss sale or disposition (including the sale date, 61 calendar days in total) may trigger the loss-deduction limit in [IRC §1091](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-124/t26/s1091/). It is not a trading ban and not a guaranteed tax exemption; the general basis adjustment of the replacement securities etc. follows IRS rules ([Pub. 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550)), and you cannot claim every case is merely a deferral.

**Wash sale: the loss is postponed, not lost (checked 2026-11-10).** [IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550): "If your loss was disallowed because of the wash sale rules, add the disallowed loss to the cost of the new stock or securities." The holding period of the new shares also includes that of the shares sold. In the IRS example, you buy 100 shares for $1,000, sell them for $750, and buy 100 again within 30 days for $800: the $250 loss is disallowed and the new basis is $1,050. If you buy more or fewer shares than you sold, Pub 550 matches the replacement shares bought within 30 days **before or after** the sale to the shares sold, in the order you bought them. Exceptions: if the replacement is bought inside your IRA or Roth IRA, the disallowed loss is **not** added to any basis; a purchase by your spouse or by a corporation you control is also a wash sale. This course's backtests stay **pre-tax**; an after-tax simulation would need tax-lot accounting and a 61-day check for every lot. Whether any of this applies to you is not decided here (see the identity note above).

**US capital gains: more than one year makes it long-term (checked 2026-11-10; general information, not tax advice).** For a US taxpayer, a gain or loss on stock held more than one year is long-term, and one held one year or less is short-term. Count from the day after you bought through the day you sold ([IRS Topic 409](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409)). For example, a stock bought on 2025-03-10 and sold on 2026-03-10 has been held exactly one year, which is not "more than one year", so the result is short-term. Selling on 2026-03-11 makes it long-term. Net short-term gains are taxed at ordinary income rates. Long-term gains usually face lower rates; the rates and thresholds change each year, so check the IRS page. Dividends have a similar test. To be a "qualified dividend", the stock generally must be held more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date, counting the sale day but not the purchase day ([IRS Pub 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550)). Rules differ if you are not a US tax resident. IRS Pub 519 says a nonresident alien present in the US for less than 183 days in the year is generally exempt from US tax on capital gains, unless the gains are effectively connected with a US trade or business. Dividends are generally withheld at 30% or a lower treaty rate ([IRS Pub 519](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p519)). Read this alongside the W-8BEN note above. This lesson does not decide which status applies to you, and Chinese tax residents also need China's individual income tax rules (below). Backtests report pre-tax returns by default. If you report after-tax returns, state whose taxes and which assumptions you used.

**If capital losses exceed capital gains, the deductible amount against income is limited to $3,000 a year ($1,500 if married filing a separate return); unused losses carry forward and keep their long-term or short-term character (checked 2026-11-12; general information, not tax advice).** The “more than one year” long-term holding period is already taught above. This is the next official Publication 550 fact. [IRS Publication 550 (2025)](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550), “Capital Losses”: “If your capital losses are more than your capital gains, you can claim a capital loss deduction.” “**Limit on deduction.** Your allowable capital loss deduction, figured on Schedule D (Form 1040), is the lesser of: • $3,000 ($1,500 if you are married and file a separate return), or • Your total net loss as shown on line 16 of Schedule D (Form 1040). You can use your total net loss to reduce your income dollar for dollar, up to the $3,000 limit.” “**Capital loss carryover.** … you can carry over the unused part to the next year and treat it as if you had incurred it in that next year. … When you carry over a loss, it remains long-term or short-term.” Official example: “Bob and Shelly sold securities in 2025. The sales resulted in a capital loss of $7,000. They had no other capital transactions. Their taxable income was $26,000. On their joint 2025 return, they can deduct $3,000. The unused part of the loss, $4,000 ($7,000 − $3,000), can be carried over to 2026. If their capital loss had been $2,000, their capital loss deduction would have been $2,000. They would have no carryover.”

Do not write that a $7,000 net capital loss all hits the same year’s taxable income. Do not invent a different cap for short-term vs long-term (the cap is on the total net loss; carryovers keep their character). Do not forget the $1,500 married-filing-separately figure. This lesson does not compute a named person’s tax, recommend a realization strategy, or treat this as advice. **Which lot did you sell? FIFO unless you identify it (checked 2026-11-11; general information, not tax advice).** If you bought the same stock several times at different prices, each purchase is a **lot**. When you sell part of the position, the cost basis depends on which lots count as sold. [IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550) ("Identifying stock or bonds sold"): if you can **adequately identify** the shares sold, their basis is the cost of those particular shares. When your broker holds the stock, adequate identification means you tell the broker which shares to sell at the time of the sale **and** receive a written confirmation within a reasonable time. Under [Treas. Reg. 1.1012-1(c)(8)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/section-1.1012-1), the identification must be made no later than the earlier of the settlement date or the settlement time required by Rule 15c6-1, and a **standing order or instruction** given in advance (for example "always sell the highest-cost lot first") counts as adequate identification made at the time of sale. If you cannot identify them, "the basis of the securities you sell is the basis of the securities you acquired first" (first in, first out, FIFO), and, except for shares of a mutual fund or other regulated investment company (RIC) and shares acquired after December 31, 2010 through a dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP) and left on deposit with a custodian, you cannot use the average price per share ([26 CFR 1.1012-1(e)(1)(i)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/section-1.1012-1); Pub 550 says "after 2011", which does not match the regulation, so follow the regulation). A **covered security** is one whose basis your broker must report on Form 1099-B: generally stock acquired on or after 2011-01-01, and fund or DRIP shares eligible for average basis acquired on or after 2012-01-01 ([26 CFR 1.6045-1(a)(15)(i)(A)(B)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/section-1.6045-1)). For a backtest or paper ledger this means: store every lot (date, shares, cost), not just an average cost, and record the lot method next to every sale.

## Constructive sales, Section 1256 60/40, and return of capital (checked 2026-11-12)

The next three points are US tax literacy only. They are **not tax advice**, and this lesson does not decide whether they apply to you. Backtests stay pre-tax unless labelled otherwise.

**1. A constructive sale (IRC §1259) is not a wash sale, and not Section 16(b).** If you hold an appreciated financial position (stock, certain debt, or a partnership interest that would produce gain if sold) and you enter certain hedges — for example a short sale of the same or **substantially identical** property, an offsetting notional principal contract, or a futures or forward contract to deliver the same or substantially identical property — that can be a [constructive sale](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1259) ([26 U.S.C. §1259](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1259); [IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550), “Constructive Sales of Appreciated Financial Positions”). You **recognize gain as if the position were sold at FMV on that date**; the holding period of the position starts over on that date; later actual close-out is adjusted for the gain already taken into account (§1259(a), (c)). This is not a trading ban, and it is not a rule that hands the profit to the issuer. Not every hedge is a constructive sale: Pub 550 / §1259(b) exclude, among others, positions whose appreciation is already accounted for under mark-to-market, including Section 1256 contracts, and the statute has a closed-transaction exception — do not teach a homemade “any short against a long is always 1259” rule.

**2. Section 1256 60/40 is not the tax treatment of ordinary equity options.** A Section 1256 contract includes a regulated futures contract, a foreign currency contract, a **nonequity option**, a dealer equity option, or a dealer securities futures contract (Pub 550; [IRC §1256](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1256)). A **nonequity option** is a listed option that is not an equity option and “include[s] … **broad-based stock index options**” (Pub 550 example: S&P 500). An **equity option** is an option to buy or sell stock, or one valued by reference to any stock or a **narrow-based** index — so ordinary single-stock equity options are not 1256 contracts. If you hold a 1256 contract at year-end, you generally treat it as sold at FMV on the last business day of the year (mark-to-market). Under the 60/40 rule, **60% of the capital gain or loss is long-term and 40% is short-term, regardless of how long you actually held the property.** Do not apply 60/40 to single-stock (or narrow-based) equity options; do not call every index product 1256 — Pub 550 says a cash-settled index option is a nonequity option if it is traded on or subject to the rules of a qualified board of exchange and the SEC has determined the index is broad-based. That ties to the existing “index options may be European-style and cash-settled; read the specification” sentence; it is not a product recommendation.

**3. Return of capital is not a dividend.** A **nondividend distribution** “is a distribution that is not paid out of the earnings and profits of a corporation or a mutual fund.” It appears in **Form 1099-DIV, box 3**. “A nondividend distribution **reduces the basis of your stock**. It is not taxed until your basis in the stock is fully recovered. This nontaxable portion also is called a **return of capital**.” After basis is zero, further nondividend distributions are reported as capital gain; long-term vs short-term follows the holding period of the stock (Pub 550, with the $100 / $80 / $30 example: basis goes 100 → 20 → 0, and $10 is long-term gain). If lots cannot be identified, reduce the earliest purchases first. Do not treat every cash distribution as a qualified dividend (the 60/121-day test already taught does not apply to box 3); do not ignore the basis reduction, or the price drop on the ex-date will be double-counted or mis-labelled. The FINRA 11140(b) 25%+ ex-date deferral remains a *when-the-price-drops* rule, not a tax-character rule. The dividend-dates section in `data-cleaning` has a one-line pointer to this discussion.

## Short over the ex-date, payments in lieu, and preferred-stock QDI (checked 2026-11-12)

The holding-period section already taught the common-stock qualified-dividend test: more than 60 days in the 121-day window beginning 60 days before the ex-date. The return-of-capital section already taught that Form 1099-DIV box 3 is not a dividend. Two official facts remain, taught together because they are the main “this cash look-alike is not a qualified dividend” traps left after that. This is **general information, not tax advice**, and this lesson does not decide whether it applies to you.

**1. P&L: a short over the ex-date has a cash dividend obligation.** The investor.gov bulletin “[An Introduction to Short Sales](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins-51)”, updated **2026-09-09**, states: “If the stock you borrow **pays a dividend, you must pay the dividend** to the person or firm making the loan.” A short backtest that “pays no dividends” (already listed as a trap in `pairs-stat-arb`) is omitting a cash cost.

**2. Tax character of substitute payments.** IRS Publication 550 (2025), “Reporting Substitute Payments”: if a broker “transferred your securities for use in a short sale or similar transaction and received certain **substitute dividend payments** on your behalf,” it must report them on **Form 1099-MISC** (or a similar statement). “**Do not treat these substitute payments as dividends or interest.** Instead, report the substitute payments shown on Form 1099-MISC as ‘Other income’…” A substitute payment in lieu of a dividend is one whose “ex-dividend date is after the transfer of stock for use in a short sale and before the closing of the short sale.” Under “Dividends that are not qualified dividends,” Pub 550 also lists, among others, “**Dividends on any share of stock to the extent you are obligated (whether under a short sale or otherwise) to make related payments** for positions in substantially similar or related property,” and “**Payments in lieu of dividends**, but only if you know or have reason to know the payments are not qualified dividends.” Those items “are not qualified dividends **even if they are shown on Form 1099-DIV, box 1b**.”

**3. Preferred stock uses 90/181 only when the dividends are due to periods totaling more than 366 days.** The ordinary QDI holding period is more than 60 days in the 121-day window. “**Exception for preferred stock.** In the case of preferred stock, you must have held the stock **more than 90 days** during the **181-day** period that begins 90 days before the ex-dividend date **if the dividends are due to periods totaling more than 366 days**.” If the preferred dividends are due to periods totaling less than 367 days, the ordinary 60/121 test applies. Do not apply 90/181 to common stock, and do not call every preferred dividend a 90-day test. Do not add a blunt “REIT dividends are never QDI” claim: “qualified REIT dividends” in the 1099-DIV instructions are an IRC §199A / box 5 concept, defined as a REIT dividend that is *not* a capital-gain dividend or a qualified dividend. A REIT can still designate a box 1b qualified-dividend portion.

**A short sale’s tax gain or loss is not realized until the short is closed, and the long-term / short-term character generally follows the holding period of the property delivered to close it — which can be less than one day even if the short was open for more than a year (checked 2026-11-12; general information, not tax advice).** Short-over-ex-div cash obligation and payments in lieu (not QDI) are already taught above. Constructive sales under §1259 are already taught as a different rule. This is the next official Publication 550 short-sale fact. [IRS Publication 550 (2025)](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550), “Short Sales”: “A short sale occurs when you agree to sell property you do not own (or own but do not wish to sell).” “You do **not realize gain or loss until delivery** of property to close the short sale.” “**As a general rule**, you determine whether you have short-term or long-term capital gain or loss on a short sale **by the amount of time you actually hold the property that’s eventually delivered to the lender to close the short sale**.” Official example: “Even though you do not own any stock of Ace Corporation, you contract to sell 100 shares of it, which you borrow from your broker. ## Form 13H: crossing the large-trader identifying-activity level is a confidential identification filing (checked 2026-11-12)

This is a **compliance-identification** box, not a public filing-lag item. Do not add Form 13H to the Form 4 / 13D / 13G / 13F table in `data-cleaning`. It is not a tape or EDGAR point-in-time input.

Under [17 CFR 240.13h-1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-240.13h-1), a large trader is a person that, directly or indirectly (including through persons it controls), **exercises investment discretion** over one or more accounts and effects purchases or sales of **any NMS security** through registered broker-dealers in an aggregate amount equal to or greater than the identifying activity level — or that files voluntarily. The identifying activity level is aggregate transactions in NMS securities equal to or greater than: during a **calendar day**, either **two million shares** or shares with a fair market value of **$20 million**; or during a **calendar month**, either **twenty million shares** or **$200 million**.

The [SEC large-trader FAQ](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/staff-guidance/trading-markets-frequently-asked-questions/responses-frequently-0) (Q1.2, last reviewed or updated 2016-02-22) states that the test is aggregate trading in **all NMS securities**, and that “transactions **not be netted** within or among accounts.” The rule says the same in 13h-1(c)(1). Options: count the **underlying** volume or fair market value of equity-option transactions; for index options, aggregate the **fair market value** of the option transactions; **exclude** purchases or sales of the underlying “pursuant to exercises or assignments of option contracts.”

After first crossing the level, file Form 13H **promptly**. The official [Form 13H](https://www.sec.gov/files/form13h.pdf) instructions and the rule use only “promptly.” This lesson does **not** invent an unofficial “promptly = 10 days” clock — it is not in the Form, the rule, or the SEC FAQ. Then file **annually within 45 days** after each full calendar year, and file an amended Form **promptly following the end of a calendar quarter** if information becomes inaccurate. Disclose the **LTID** and the accounts to which it applies to each broker effecting transactions. The Form states the Commission “will treat as confidential the information collected pursuant to this Form” (limited statutory exceptions).

A scaled backtest or live book that ignores the threshold is a compliance gap, not a data-lag gap. No registration or broker recommendation.

### United States — SEC "Innovation Exemption" for tokenized NMS stock (checked 2026-11-10)

Three terms first. An **NMS stock** is, roughly, a stock listed on a US national securities exchange (the kind Regulation NMS covers). A **tokenized** NMS stock is that stock recorded as a token on a blockchain. A **TSV** (Tokenized Securities Venue) is an on-chain venue that brings buyers and sellers of such tokens together through an **AMM** (automated market maker: a pool of two assets that prices each trade by formula, not by an order book).

On 2026-09-17 the SEC issued an order granting **temporary, conditional** exemptions ([Release 34-106402, File 4-927](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/exorders/2026/34-106402.pdf); published in the Federal Register on 2026-09-22 as [91 FR 60168](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/22/2026-19388/order-granting-temporary-conditional-exemptive-relief-pursuant-to-section-36a1-of-the-securities)). A TSV that meets the conditions is exempt from the definition of "exchange", and liquidity providers that supply tokenized NMS stock to a TSV's AMM pool with their own capital ("Covered Firms") can be exempt from the definition of "dealer". The anti-fraud and anti-manipulation provisions still apply in full ([Atkins statement](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/atkins-innovation-exemption-bridge-toward-durable-rulemaking-091726)).

Other conditions named in the order and [press release 2026-90](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2026-90-sec-issues-innovation-exemption-facilitate-trading-tokenized-nms-stock-request-comment): the token must give holders the same rights and privileges as the traditional stock of the same class (the Atkins statement names dividends and voting rights); tokens that only give synthetic exposure, such as a tokenized security-based swap, are excluded; before trading a stock tokenized by an unaffiliated third party, the TSV must give the issuer written notice and a chance to object; smart contracts must be auditable, public and deployed on a public, permissionless ledger; the TSV must stop trading a token whenever the primary listing exchange stops trading the underlying stock; and the TSV must publish notices about its operations and trading. The Atkins statement adds that a TSV must be a US person that complies with OFAC sanctions programs and that access is permissioned. The [Uyeda statement](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/uyeda-statement-innovation-exemption-091726) says USD transaction data (price, size, time, pool address, end-of-day pool size, daily volume) will be public.

## Common mistakes / pitfalls

- Treating the ESMA briefing as a tick-box license checklist or HFT playbook.

- Assuming PDA withdrawal erased all AI/advice duties (FY2026 exams still list Emerging FinTech).
- Treating FINRA GenAI themes as a trading license or product alpha.
- Treating SFC 24EC55 as a trading license rather than licensed-corporation expectations.
- Mixing up a wash sale (tax) with a wash trade (manipulation); treating a toy self-trade or pattern flag as a legal finding; treating a swapped event order that the toy no longer flags as a compliance approach.

1. Quoting blog thresholds instead of exchange/CSRC text.
2. Treating staff statements as final Commission rules.
3. Ignoring that PDT/$25k was replaced (Notice 26-10 / 26-11 IMD/IML) if teaching US retail margin in 2026+.
4. Conflating securities 〔2024〕8 with futures 〔2025〕12 — markets and exchanges differ.
5. Offering personalised trading advice from this course.
6. Skipping the “date checked” stamp.
7. Applying today’s trading rules to the whole historical backtest; treating still-deferred provisions as in force.

9. Saying a wash-sale loss is "gone forever": in a taxable account it is added to the new shares' basis and shows up when they are sold (an IRA/Roth repurchase is the exception); checking only purchases after the sale.
