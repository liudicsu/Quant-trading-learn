## Where the risk-free rate comes from (checked 2026-11-10)

The Sharpe ratio above uses Rf = 0, which is a teaching shortcut. In real research the numerator is the excess return: the strategy's return minus the risk-free rate over the same period. US Treasury bill (T-bill) rates are a common teaching example, not the only correct choice; whatever proxy you use, write it down. Watch three things.

1. **The quoted discount rate is not your yield.** T-bills are sold at a discount and pay face value at maturity. The quoted "discount rate" uses a 360-day year and divides by face value, not by the price you pay. TreasuryDirect gives the price formula: price = face value × (1 − discount rate × days ÷ 360) ([TreasuryDirect](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/understanding-pricing/)). In the lab, a 4.00% discount rate on a 91-day bill gives a price of 989.8889, and the simple 365-day yield from that price is 4.0970%, higher than 4.00%. Treasury's daily table has both a "BANK DISCOUNT" and a "COUPON EQUIVALENT" column; pick one and say which.
2. **Point in time.** Treasury's daily bill rates are quotes the New York Fed obtains at about 3:30 PM Eastern each business day ([Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/policy-issues/financing-the-government/interest-rate-statistics)), so day t's rate can only be applied to day t+1's return.
3. **Frequency.** Convert the annual rate to the same frequency as your returns and say how you did it. In the lab, with a daily mean of 0.05% and daily volatility of 1%, Sharpe is 0.7937 at Rf = 0 and 0.5417 with a 4% rate spread over 252 days.

**SOFR is published at approximately 8:00 a.m. ET, one business day after its value date (checked 2026-11-12).** The T-bill point-in-time rule above stays as already taught: a ~15:30 ET quote on day t applies from day t+1. Do not rewrite that paragraph, and do not copy that sentence onto SOFR.

The New York Fed [SOFR](https://www.newyorkfed.org/markets/reference-rates/sofr) page: “Each business day, the New York Fed publishes the SOFR on the New York Fed website at **approximately 8:00 a.m. ET**.” Official [Additional Information about Reference Rates](https://www.newyorkfed.org/markets/reference-rates/additional-information-about-reference-rates): “the **SOFR is published one business day following its value date** (the day when overnight trades were negotiated).” Official example on the same page: “The first SOFR was published on 4/3/2018 for the value date of 4/2/2018.” Same-day revisions “may be revised at **approximately 2:30 p.m. ET**” and “will only occur if the change in the rate exceeds one basis point and only on the same day as initial publication.” Contrast, do not conflate: the **EFFR and OBFR** “will be published at approximately **9:00 a.m. ET** each business day” that is not on the NY Fed holiday schedule.

**Look-ahead:** regular US equity trading opens at 09:30 ET, so the SOFR published at about 8:00 a.m. today — which is the **prior business day’s** value date — is already out and can be used at that day’s regular open. The SOFR whose value date is *today* is published at about 8:00 a.m. on the *next* business day and cannot be used this morning. Do not treat 8:00 a.m. as after the 9:30 open. Do not write that every NY Fed reference rate is published at 8:00 a.m. Do not invent a G.19 clock, a first-Friday rule, or a SOFR trading strategy. SOFR is a teaching rate timestamp, not “the” correct risk-free rate; whatever proxy you use, write it down.

## Leveraged and inverse ETFs reset daily (checked 2026-11-10)

**Long-run returns are not "index × multiple".** A 2x leveraged ETF aims for twice the index's return each day. An inverse ETF aims for the opposite of the index's return each day. The SEC's investor bulletin gives an example. An index falls 10% from 1000 to 900 on day 1, then rises 10% to 990 on day 2, for a two-day loss of 1%. A 2x ETF falls 20% to 800, then rises 20% to 960, for a two-day loss of 4%. It hit its target on both days, yet over two days it lost four times what the index lost, not twice ([SEC investor bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-alerts/sec)). In choppy markets the gap keeps growing, which is the same path dependence you saw with max drawdown above. To backtest holding a leveraged ETF for longer than a day, compound daily; never multiply the index's cumulative return. Real funds also carry fees and financing costs that the toy below leaves out. This is why the site's default research market says "non-leveraged ETFs".

## Common mistakes / pitfalls

1. Annualizing vol with ×252 instead of √252.
2. Comparing Sharpes without stating Rf/frequency.
3. Reporting returns without MDD.
4. Letting full-sample corr hide stress corr spikes.
5. Treating tiny rolling windows as precise truth.
6. Backtesting a 2x or inverse ETF held for weeks as "index return × 2" instead of compounding daily.
7. Using a T-bill's quoted discount rate as its yield; subtracting day t's rate (quoted about 15:30 ET) from day t's return.
8. Copying the T-bill “~15:30 ET quote on day t applies from day t+1” sentence onto SOFR; treating 8:00 a.m. as after the 9:30 open; writing that every NY Fed reference rate is published at 8:00 a.m.
