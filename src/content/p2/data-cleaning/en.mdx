## Why `panel_nav_honest_shift1` equals `signal × next_ret`

Those two NAV prints are equal because they are the **same PnL products shifted by one timestamp**: `shift(1)×ret` vs `signal×ret.shift(-1)`. Equal NAV is **not** a look-ahead test — both already use next-period returns with a prior signal. Same-bar `signal×ret` would be look-ahead. (See lab print `equality_why`.)

## Information bars vs time bars (AFML ch.2)

**Intuition:** A **time bar** samples the wall clock (every minute, every day). Markets do not speak at a constant rate: a quiet hour and a news burst both become “one bar,” so return variance and microstructure noise look uneven. **Tick / volume / dollar bars** sample *activity* instead — emit a bar after \(N\) trades, \(V\) shares, or \(D\) dollars of notional. Busy periods mint more bars; quiet periods fewer. That is a **research sampling** choice for cleaner statistical clocks — **not** an HFT latency or execution recipe.

**Toy comparison** on one synthetic 5,000-tick tape (`examples/p2/01_data_cleaning.py`):

| Clock | n_bars | mean \|ret\| | std(ret) |
|-------|--------|-------------|----------|
| time/tick every 50 | 100 | ≈0.0045 | ≈0.0058 |
| volume 200 shares | 241 | ≈0.0030 | ≈0.0036 |
| dollar ≈200×mid | 231 | ≈0.0030 | ≈0.0038 |

Activity clocks produce **more** bars with **smaller** per-bar moves on the same tape — the series is closer to an “information clock.”



## Mask-first / upstream contamination (A-share comparison case: limit closes in rolling windows; US analogue: halted / LULD-paused / no-quote rows)

[arXiv:2507.07107](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07107) documents a costly A-share pipeline failure: the authors treat limit-up/down closes as **non-executable for their strategy**, yet those closes enter rolling means / correlations / ranks **before** any row filter — **upstream contamination**. **This synthetic toy deliberately sets** those rows as non-executable for the given side/size; the conservative mask is an **experimental protocol**, not a market law that “touching the limit means no fill” (whether a fill happens at a limit depends on side, queue size and counterparties).

**Paper ablation (warning numbers only — not course alpha):** removing the mask **inflates apparent IC by ~+18%** while **cutting realised Sharpe by ~0.44** — the model learns returns it cannot trade.

**Keep three things apart (R33):**

- **Information availability (observation):** was the value public and visible at decision time?
- **Information quality:** is the value clean (not a bad print, not stale, not a filled-in gap)?
- **Executability (tradability, by side and size):** given the session, quotes, trading status and liquidity at that time, could an order of **this side and this size** be filled?

Hitting a limit or a pause affects only the third question, and it depends on side and size; it does not by itself mean the price “did not exist” or “cannot be used as information”. Whether a public, quality-checked historical trade price enters the features is decided by the research protocol; “cannot order now” does not imply “information unavailable”. The US analogue is an explicit state such as **no matching during a pause / no executable quote**, not erasing legitimate prices printed before the pause.

**Boolean mask-first (this toy’s optional conservative protocol):**

1. At load time build `obs[t,i]` (public at decision time), `quality[t,i]` (clean) and `trade[t,i,side,size]` (toy setting: not halted ∧ not at the limit on that side ∧ past seasoning) separately; the toy optionally combines them into a conservative `M[t,i]` (see the three-mask lab below).
2. Every rolling operator reads **only** cells with `M=1`; any `M=0` in the window → mark output unreliable (conservative).
3. **Post-hoc row drops cannot un-poison** aggregates already computed.

## Data versions, corporate actions & trading calendars

**Intuition:** the same stock over the same period can show a different monthly return under a different “data version” or calculation convention. A backtest number is reproducible only if you write down **which version you used**.

**Real example (verified on Ken French’s official site):** the [Ken French Data Library](https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html) states that CRSP’s Legacy (FIZ) files were discontinued after the December 2024 data release; **beginning with the January 2025 data release**, US research returns are generated from the new Flat File Format 2.0 (CIZ). The site highlights: FIZ monthly returns are “month to month holding period returns with dividends reinvested at month-end”; CIZ monthly returns are “**compounded daily returns** with dividends reinvested on their **ex-dates**.” The site also keeps historical data based on the legacy FIZ format.

**Formula:** with daily total return `r_d = (P_d + D_d) / P_(d−1) − 1` (`D_d` non-zero only on the ex-date), the CIZ-style monthly return is `R_m = Π_(d in m) (1 + r_d) − 1`; the month-end-reinvest style is roughly `(P_end + D) / P_start − 1`.

**Numeric example (`examples/p2/05_data_versions_calendar.py`, seed=7, synthetic):** the same prices and one dividend of 2.0:

```text
Jan month-end reinvest  : -0.102533
Jan ex-date reinvest    : -0.104362
difference (bp)         : -18.28
```

The two conventions differ by about 18 bp — the data are not wrong; the **convention differs**.

**Corporate action (split):** on a 2-for-1 split day the raw price goes from 51.0 to 25.6, producing a fake −49.8% “crash”; the adjusted return that day is only +0.39%:

```text
raw daily ret     : [0.02, -0.498, 0.0117]
adjusted daily ret: [0.02, 0.0039, 0.0117]
```

**Trading calendar:** two markets have different holidays (market B is closed on 04-04). Forward-filling B’s **returns** (ffill) “invents” a return on a closed day; align by each market’s own calendar, or use only the intersection of open days:

```text
A days=8 B days=7 inner-join days=7
ffill rows where B return is a stale copy: 1
```

**Practical rule:** the data sheet states: source + version/release month + return convention (when dividends are reinvested) + adjustment method + trading calendar used. After a version change, rerun the old result first, then compare.

## When is a financial report "known"? Filing deadlines and EDGAR filing dates (checked 2026-11-10)

**The period-end date is not the date you knew the numbers.** On the last day of a quarter, nobody has that quarter's report yet. Per the SEC form instructions, an annual report on Form 10-K is due 60 days after fiscal year-end for large accelerated filers, 75 days for accelerated filers and 90 days for everyone else ([Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/files/form10-k.pdf)); the filer categories are SEC size tiers based mainly on the market value of shares held by the public. A quarterly report on Form 10-Q is due in 40 days (large accelerated and accelerated filers) or 45 days (everyone else) ([Form 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/files/form10-q.pdf)). A current report on Form 8-K, for events such as an acquisition or a CEO change, is generally due within four business days of the event ([investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/form-8-k)). EDGAR (the SEC's filing system) also treats a filing whose transmission starts after 5:30 p.m. Eastern as filed on the next business day ([Reg S-T Rule 13](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-232.13); insider Forms 3/4/5, Schedules 13D/13G and Form 144 get until 10 p.m.). The official "filing date" and the acceptance time can therefore differ by a day, so store both. In a backtest, use the acceptance timestamp when you have it, and act no earlier than the next tradable moment after it (this is what the EDGAR-shaped as-of join in the lab above does). If all you have is the period-end date, push it forward by at least the statutory deadline; never use the period-end date itself. Deadlines are upper bounds: many companies file earlier, so this fallback is conservative, not exact. Institutional holdings on Form 13F (managers with at least $100 million in certain US securities) are due within 45 days after quarter-end; for 3Q 2026 that is 2026-11-16 ([SEC FAQ](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/staff-guidance/division-investment-management-frequently-asked-questions/frequently-asked-questions-about-form-13f)). A 13F-based signal becomes usable on the filing date, not the quarter-end. Status: on 2026-05-05 the SEC proposed letting companies file semiannual reports on a new Form 10-S instead of quarterly 10-Qs ([SEC press release](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2026-42-sec-proposes-amendments-permit-optional-semiannual-reporting-public-companies)). It was proposed on 2026-05-05 and is not adopted as of 2026-11-10, so quarterly reporting is unchanged.

## Insider and large-holder filings: when are they "known"? (checked 2026-11-10)

Directors, officers and holders of more than 10% of a company's stock ("insiders") report their trades in that company's stock on Form 4 ([investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/forms-3-4-and-5)). The form is due before the end of the second business day after the date of execution; for certain planned transactions the clock starts on the deemed execution date under 16a-3(g)(4) ([17 CFR 240.16a-3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-240.16a-3)). So a Friday trade may not appear until Tuesday. An investor who goes above 5% generally files Schedule 13D within five business days and amends it within two business days after a material change. Qualifying institutions may file the shorter Schedule 13G instead, for example within 45 days after quarter-end; 13G deadlines differ by type of filer ([17 CFR 240.13d-1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-240.13d-1); [SEC 2023-219](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2023-219)).

In a backtest, a signal becomes usable once EDGAR has accepted the filing, not on the transaction date printed in the form. Using the transaction date usually looks up to two business days into the future; it can be longer for deemed execution dates (16a-3(g)(4)) or late filings. A filing accepted at 21:40 ET is on time for that day, but the regular session already closed at 16:00, so a daily backtest can act at the next session at the earliest.

One 2026 change: since 2026-03-18, directors and officers of foreign private issuers that have equity securities registered under Section 12 must also file Form 4, but the SEC has granted exemptive orders for several jurisdictions, including Canada, the European Economic Area, the UK and Australia ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/about/divisions-offices/division-corporation-finance/holding-foreign-insiders-accountable-act-section-16a-reporting-requirements)); the list may change, so read the SEC page rather than copying it. For these companies, "no Form 4 before 2026-03-18" does not mean insiders did not trade; they simply did not have to report. After that date, check the exemption list first.

## Raw vs adjusted prices and total return; delisting

**Raw prices (per review item R21):** raw (unadjusted) prices are used for the historical nominal price scale and the share/cash ledger; whether a strategy could trade at a given price and size still depends on the session, quotes, trading status, liquidity and the order/fill model at that time — raw OHLC or the last trade price is not automatically executable. Split-adjusted and total-return series each record their adjustment scope; never count the same dividend twice, and a split adjusts the share count too rather than creating wealth out of nothing.

## Delisting returns and permanent IDs (CRSP CIZ) (checked 2026-11-10)

**A delisting is not "end of data"; it is a return you must book.** After a stock delists, your final outcome is not necessarily the last trade price. It may trade over the counter, pay a liquidation or merger consideration, or go to zero. CRSP (the Center for Research in Security Prices; part of [Morningstar](https://newsroom.morningstar.com/news/news-details/2026/Morningstar-Completes-Acquisition-of-CRSP-and-Extends-Relationship-with-Vanguard/default.aspx) since 2026-02-02) calls the return from the last trade price to the post-delisting value the delisting return. Per the CRSP CIZ guide (July 2026 edition; product page: [CRSP US stock databases](https://www.crsp.org/research/crsp-us-stock-databases/)), the monthly return MthRet already includes delisting returns where appropriate, so do not apply them a second time. It excludes them in two flagged cases: the post-delisting value comes more than 10 trading days after delisting (flag G), or the delisting return is missing (flag M). You must decide how to treat those two cases and document the choice. Shumway (1997, *Journal of Finance*) found that most stocks delisted for bankruptcy and other negative reasons lack a correct delisting return, and that the omitted returns are large ([doi:10.1111/j.1540-6261.1997.tb03818.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1997.tb03818.x)). Dropping those stocks, or treating missing values as zero, makes a backtest look too good. Also, tickers change and get reused by other companies. Key your data on a permanent ID such as PERMNO (CRSP's permanent security identifier) or CIK (the SEC's company identifier), and store the ticker as a time-varying attribute.

## Reverse splits and Nasdaq's $1 bid-price rule: listing status as data (checked 2026-11-11)

**Why a data lesson cares.** A stock that drifts below $1 on Nasdaq starts a listing clock. Companies often answer with a **reverse split** (for example 10 old shares become 1 new share, so the price is 10 times higher). In a raw price series that shows up as a huge fake jump, and the stock may still be delisted later. Both the split and the listing status need to be in your data.

**The rules, in order (Nasdaq Rule 5810(c)(3)(A), as described in SEC orders):**

1. **Failure:** the closing bid is below $1.00 for **30 consecutive business days**. The company then gets a compliance period of **180 calendar days** from Nasdaq's notice; some Nasdaq Capital Market companies can get a second 180 days. It regains compliance with a closing bid of $1.00 or more for at least **10 consecutive business days** ([SEC 34-102245](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nasdaq/2025/34-102245.pdf), 2025-01-17).
2. **Recent reverse splits:** if the company effected a reverse split over the prior one-year period, or effected one or more reverse splits over the prior two-year period with a cumulative ratio of **250 shares or more to one**, it gets **no** compliance period and Nasdaq issues a delisting determination (Rule 5810(c)(3)(A)(iv), [SEC 34-100767 Exhibit 5](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nasdaq/2024/34-100767-ex5.pdf); see also 34-102245). A company can still appeal to a Hearings Panel.
3. **$0.10 rule:** a closing bid of $0.10 or less for ten consecutive business days leads to a delisting determination and **suspension** of trading on Nasdaq, with no compliance period. The SEC approved this on 2025-12-05, and it became operative 45 days later ([SEC 34-104318, 90 FR 57225](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/12/10/2025-22398/self-regulatory-organizations-the-nasdaq-stock-market-llc-notice-of-filing-of-amendment-no-1-and)).
4. **A rule that is approved but not in force:** a new $5 million Market Value of Listed Securities requirement was approved on 2026-07-22 ([SEC 34-105971](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nasdaq/2026/34-105971.pdf)), then automatically stayed on 2026-07-29, when notices of intention to petition for review were filed (SEC Rule of Practice 431(e)). On 2026-09-11 the Commission granted review and kept the approval order stayed "pending further order" ([SEC 34-106338](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/other/2026/34-106338.pdf)). Nasdaq filed on 2026-09-25, and the SEC noticed it on 2026-10-05 in 34-106594, moving the effective date to after the stay is lifted ([SEC 34-106594](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nasdaq/2026/34-106594.pdf)). Do not apply it in a backtest until the SEC lifts the stay.

**Backtest rules:** adjust the split day with the split ratio (see the corporate-actions section above); keep a `listing_status` column with the dates of the deficiency notice, delisting determination and suspension; when a stock is suspended and moves to the over-the-counter market, book the delisting return (see the CIZ section) instead of dropping it. These are listing rules, not trading signals; this lesson does not claim that reverse splits predict returns. NYSE has its own, different price rules, not covered here.

## Spin-offs and mergers: the fake crash and the sudden disappearance (checked 2026-11-10)

In a spin-off, a parent company distributes shares of a subsidiary to its own shareholders, usually pro rata, and the subsidiary becomes a separate company ([investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/spin-offs)). Once the new company trades, the parent's share price is adjusted lower, because its value no longer includes the spun-off unit ([FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/corporate-spinoffs)).

If you compute returns from raw closes, the ex-distribution day shows a fake crash. In the lab below the parent falls from 100 to 81, a raw return of −19%. But each parent share also received 0.5 child shares that closed at 40, so the holder's actual return is +1%. CRSP's calculations guide treats spin-offs like splits and stock dividends: they are "split events" handled with a price adjustment factor on the ex-distribution date. Shares and volume are adjusted only for splits and stock dividends, not for spin-offs. The newly received security is linked back to the parent through DisPERMNO in the distribution records.

Mergers need the same care: an acquired stock delists, and its final return comes from the merger consideration, so do not drop it as "end of data" (read this with the delisting section above). Timing matters too. The issuer reports the effect on shareholders' cost basis on IRS Form 8937, due by the 45th day after the action or January 15 of the next year, whichever is earlier ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8937)). Those official numbers can therefore appear more than a month after the ex-date. This lesson covers data and timing only, not tax treatment.

adjusted prior close   : 80.1980  adjusted return 1.0000%
volume (not adjusted for spin-offs per CRSP guide): 1000000 -> 1400000

## Dividend dates and the yfinance auto_adjust trap (checked 2026-11-10)

**1. Four dates of a cash dividend.** A company announces a dividend (declaration date), fixes who is on its books (record date), the stock starts trading without the dividend (ex-dividend date), and the cash is paid (payment date). What decides whether you get the dividend is the **ex-dividend date**: if you buy on the ex-date or later, you do not get this payment. Since US settlement moved to T+1, investor.gov says the ex-date is **usually the record date**, or one business day before it if the record date is not a business day ([investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/ex-dividend-dates-when-are-you-entitled-stock-and)). Its example: declared 2026-03-02, record 2026-03-16, ex-date 2026-03-16, paid 2026-03-17.

- **Exception:** if a cash dividend, stock dividend or split is 25% or more of the stock's value, the ex-date is deferred to one business day **after** the payment date ([FINRA Rule 11140(b)](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/11140)); someone who sells between the record date and that ex-date delivers a "due bill" for the distribution ([FINRA Rule 11630](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/11630)). For ADRs and foreign securities, FINRA designates the ex-date separately (11140(b)(3)). A stock dividend below 25% follows the normal rule: ex-date equals the record date.
- **Backtest bookkeeping:** book the total return on the **ex-date** (the price drops that day) but receive the cash on the **payment date**. Keep them separate, and never count the dividend twice. The double-count lab in section D of `05_data_versions_calendar.py` is the same idea one level down.
- **Advanced:** vendor `ex_date` fields can be missing or filled with the payment date. Check that ex-date is not after the payment date, except for cash dividends, stock dividends or splits of 25% or more.

**2. Version trap: yfinance changed what `Close` means.** yfinance 0.2.51 (released 2024-12-19) changed `yf.download()` so that `auto_adjust` defaults to `True`, matching `Ticker.history()`; 0.2.53 (2025-02-15) started warning when you do not pass it ([yfinance CHANGELOG](https://github.com/ranaroussi/yfinance/blob/main/CHANGELOG.rst)). The official docs describe the two shapes ([yfinance price adjustment](https://ranaroussi.github.io/yfinance/advanced/price_adjustment.html)):

| Call | What `Close` is | `Adj Close` column |
|---|---|---|
| `auto_adjust=True` (default since 0.2.51) | adjusted for splits **and** dividends | none |
| `auto_adjust=False` | Yahoo's price as returned, **already split-adjusted** | yes, also dividend-adjusted |

- So the same column name `Close` changes meaning across versions, and Yahoo does not give you a fully unadjusted price. That differs from this lesson's rule of using raw prices for the share and cash ledger.
- Engineering rules: pin the version, always write `auto_adjust` explicitly, check the column names, and record the field meaning in your data sheet. The current release, 1.8.0 (2026-11-03), added a price-repair fix for split-like jumps without a matching split; repair changes the data, so log whether it was on.
- yfinance remains an unofficial wrapper for personal use (see the source table above); the course does not download by default.

## Common mistakes / pitfalls

1. Backtesting a decade with today’s members only.  
2. Fundamentals without PIT.  
2b. Crypto archives that look complete but fail availability-time audits (same-bar / funding lag / backfill / unverified OI).  
3. UTC vs local off-by-one.  
4. Mixing raw/adj silently.  
5. No hash / no version.
6. Treating info bars as an HFT execution recipe rather than a research sampling clock.
7. Comparing clocks on different underlying tapes without saying so.
8. Switching data versions (e.g. FIZ→CIZ) without recording it, mistaking a convention change for a strategy change; ffilling **returns** across closed days; computing split-day returns on raw prices.
9. Treating the raw close as an executable price; treating a FRED series’ public-domain label as permission for the FRED service, caching or model training; calling yfinance official data.
10. Assuming you get the dividend if you buy on the ex-date; booking the dividend on both the ex-date and the payment date; upgrading yfinance without writing `auto_adjust` explicitly, so `Close` silently changes meaning.
11. Using the quarter-end date as the date a 10-Q or 13F was known; ignoring that EDGAR dates a filing started after 5:30 p.m. ET to the next business day.
12. Multiplying CIZ MthRet by the delisting return again, or silently setting a flag-G/M month to 0; merging histories on ticker instead of PERMNO/CIK.
13. Computing a spin-off ex-date return from raw closes (a fake −19% crash) or adjusting volume for a spin-off; dropping an acquired stock as "end of data".
14. Aligning a Form 4 signal on its transaction date instead of the EDGAR acceptance time; reading "no Form 4" for a foreign private issuer before 2026-03-18 as "insiders did not trade".
15. Computing a return across a reverse split from raw prices (a fake +900%); dropping a stock suspended under the $1 or $0.10 rule instead of booking its delisting return; applying the stayed $5 million rule as if it were in force.
