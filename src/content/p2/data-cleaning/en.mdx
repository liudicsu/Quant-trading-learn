## Why `panel_nav_honest_shift1` equals `signal × next_ret`

Those two NAV prints are equal because they are the **same PnL products shifted by one timestamp**: `shift(1)×ret` vs `signal×ret.shift(-1)`. Equal NAV is **not** a look-ahead test — both already use next-period returns with a prior signal. Same-bar `signal×ret` would be look-ahead. (See lab print `equality_why`.)

## Information bars vs time bars (AFML ch.2)

**Intuition:** A **time bar** samples the wall clock (every minute, every day). Markets do not speak at a constant rate: a quiet hour and a news burst both become “one bar,” so return variance and microstructure noise look uneven. **Tick / volume / dollar bars** sample *activity* instead — emit a bar after \(N\) trades, \(V\) shares, or \(D\) dollars of notional. Busy periods mint more bars; quiet periods fewer. That is a **research sampling** choice for cleaner statistical clocks — **not** an HFT latency or execution recipe.

**Toy comparison** on one synthetic 5,000-tick tape (`examples/p2/01_data_cleaning.py`):

| Clock | n_bars | mean \|ret\| | std(ret) |
|-------|--------|-------------|----------|
| time/tick every 50 | 100 | ≈0.0045 | ≈0.0058 |
| volume 200 shares | 241 | ≈0.0030 | ≈0.0036 |
| dollar ≈200×mid | 231 | ≈0.0030 | ≈0.0038 |

Activity clocks produce **more** bars with **smaller** per-bar moves on the same tape — the series is closer to an “information clock.”



## Mask-first / upstream contamination (A-share comparison case: limit closes in rolling windows; US analogue: halted / LULD-paused / no-quote rows)

[arXiv:2507.07107](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07107) documents a costly A-share pipeline failure: the authors treat limit-up/down closes as **non-executable for their strategy**, yet those closes enter rolling means / correlations / ranks **before** any row filter — **upstream contamination**. **This synthetic toy deliberately sets** those rows as non-executable for the given side/size; the conservative mask is an **experimental protocol**, not a market law that “touching the limit means no fill” (whether a fill happens at a limit depends on side, queue size and counterparties).

**Paper ablation (warning numbers only — not course alpha):** removing the mask **inflates apparent IC by ~+18%** while **cutting realised Sharpe by ~0.44** — the model learns returns it cannot trade.

**Keep three things apart (R33):**

- **Information availability (observation):** was the value public and visible at decision time?
- **Information quality:** is the value clean (not a bad print, not stale, not a filled-in gap)?
- **Executability (tradability, by side and size):** given the session, quotes, trading status and liquidity at that time, could an order of **this side and this size** be filled?

Hitting a limit or a pause affects only the third question, and it depends on side and size; it does not by itself mean the price “did not exist” or “cannot be used as information”. Whether a public, quality-checked historical trade price enters the features is decided by the research protocol; “cannot order now” does not imply “information unavailable”. The US analogue is an explicit state such as **no matching during a pause / no executable quote**, not erasing legitimate prices printed before the pause.

**Boolean mask-first (this toy’s optional conservative protocol):**

1. At load time build `obs[t,i]` (public at decision time), `quality[t,i]` (clean) and `trade[t,i,side,size]` (toy setting: not halted ∧ not at the limit on that side ∧ past seasoning) separately; the toy optionally combines them into a conservative `M[t,i]` (see the three-mask lab below).
2. Every rolling operator reads **only** cells with `M=1`; any `M=0` in the window → mark output unreliable (conservative).
3. **Post-hoc row drops cannot un-poison** aggregates already computed.

## Data versions, corporate actions & trading calendars

**Intuition:** the same stock over the same period can show a different monthly return under a different “data version” or calculation convention. A backtest number is reproducible only if you write down **which version you used**.

**Real example (verified on Ken French’s official site):** the [Ken French Data Library](https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html) states that CRSP’s Legacy (FIZ) files were discontinued after the December 2024 data release; **beginning with the January 2025 data release**, US research returns are generated from the new Flat File Format 2.0 (CIZ). The site highlights: FIZ monthly returns are “month to month holding period returns with dividends reinvested at month-end”; CIZ monthly returns are “**compounded daily returns** with dividends reinvested on their **ex-dates**.” The site also keeps historical data based on the legacy FIZ format.

**Formula:** with daily total return `r_d = (P_d + D_d) / P_(d−1) − 1` (`D_d` non-zero only on the ex-date), the CIZ-style monthly return is `R_m = Π_(d in m) (1 + r_d) − 1`; the month-end-reinvest style is roughly `(P_end + D) / P_start − 1`.

**Numeric example (`examples/p2/05_data_versions_calendar.py`, seed=7, synthetic):** the same prices and one dividend of 2.0:

```text
Jan month-end reinvest  : -0.102533
Jan ex-date reinvest    : -0.104362
difference (bp)         : -18.28
```

The two conventions differ by about 18 bp — the data are not wrong; the **convention differs**.

**Corporate action (split):** on a 2-for-1 split day the raw price goes from 51.0 to 25.6, producing a fake −49.8% “crash”; the adjusted return that day is only +0.39%:

```text
raw daily ret     : [0.02, -0.498, 0.0117]
adjusted daily ret: [0.02, 0.0039, 0.0117]
```

**Trading calendar:** two markets have different holidays (market B is closed on 04-04). Forward-filling B’s **returns** (ffill) “invents” a return on a closed day; align by each market’s own calendar, or use only the intersection of open days:

```text
A days=8 B days=7 inner-join days=7
ffill rows where B return is a stale copy: 1
```

**Practical rule:** the data sheet states: source + version/release month + return convention (when dividends are reinvested) + adjustment method + trading calendar used. After a version change, rerun the old result first, then compare.

## When is a financial report "known"? Filing deadlines and EDGAR filing dates (checked 2026-11-10)

**The period-end date is not the date you knew the numbers.** On the last day of a quarter, nobody has that quarter's report yet. Per the SEC form instructions, an annual report on Form 10-K is due 60 days after fiscal year-end for large accelerated filers, 75 days for accelerated filers and 90 days for everyone else ([Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/files/form10-k.pdf)); the filer categories are SEC size tiers based mainly on the market value of shares held by the public. A quarterly report on Form 10-Q is due in 40 days (large accelerated and accelerated filers) or 45 days (everyone else) ([Form 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/files/form10-q.pdf)). A current report on Form 8-K, for events such as an acquisition or a CEO change, is generally due within four business days of the event ([investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/form-8-k)). EDGAR (the SEC's filing system) also treats a filing whose transmission starts after 5:30 p.m. Eastern as filed on the next business day ([Reg S-T Rule 13](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-232.13); insider Forms 3/4/5, Schedules 13D/13G and Form 144 get until 10 p.m.). The official "filing date" and the acceptance time can therefore differ by a day, so store both. In a backtest, use the acceptance timestamp when you have it, and act no earlier than the next tradable moment after it (this is what the EDGAR-shaped as-of join in the lab above does). If all you have is the period-end date, push it forward by at least the statutory deadline; never use the period-end date itself. Deadlines are upper bounds: many companies file earlier, so this fallback is conservative, not exact. Institutional holdings on Form 13F (managers with at least $100 million in certain US securities) are due within 45 days after quarter-end; for 3Q 2026 that is 2026-11-16 ([SEC FAQ](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/staff-guidance/division-investment-management-frequently-asked-questions/frequently-asked-questions-about-form-13f)). A 13F-based signal becomes usable on the filing date, not the quarter-end. Status: on 2026-05-05 the SEC proposed letting companies file semiannual reports on a new Form 10-S instead of quarterly 10-Qs ([SEC press release](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2026-42-sec-proposes-amendments-permit-optional-semiannual-reporting-public-companies)). It was proposed on 2026-05-05 and is not adopted as of 2026-11-11, so quarterly reporting is unchanged.


**Those 60 / 75 / 90 and 40 / 45 clocks belong to official 12b-2 filer categories, with dollar thresholds, a second-quarter measurement date, and hysteresis (checked 2026-11-12).** The deadline numbers and the sentence that filer categories are “SEC size tiers based mainly on the market value of shares held by the public” stay as already taught. So does the Form 10-S proposal sentence. This section only adds the official current-law numbers. Do not edit `examples/p2/09_filing_dates_toy.py`.

Current [17 CFR 240.12b-2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-240.12b-2): an **accelerated filer**, after it first meets the conditions as of the end of its fiscal year, had “an aggregate worldwide market value of the voting and non-voting common equity held by its non-affiliates of **$75 million or more, but less than $700 million**, as of the **last business day of the issuer's most recently completed second fiscal quarter**,” has been subject to section 13(a) or 15(d) for at least **twelve calendar months**, has filed **at least one annual report**, and is not eligible for smaller-reporting-company treatment under the listed revenue test. A **large accelerated filer** is the same structure at **$700 million or more**. The year-end determination “governs the deadlines for the annual report to be filed for that fiscal year, the quarterly and annual reports to be filed for the subsequent fiscal year,” and later reports while the issuer remains in that status.

**Hysteresis:** once an accelerated filer, it remains one unless, at fiscal year-end, that public float “was **less than $60 million**” as of the last business day of the most recently completed second fiscal quarter, or it is eligible for the SRC revenue test — then it becomes a non-accelerated filer. Once a large accelerated filer, it remains one unless that public float “was **less than $560 million**” (or the SRC revenue test applies). If, after that large-accelerated test, the float was “**$60 million or more, but less than $560 million**” and the SRC revenue test does not apply, it becomes an accelerated filer; if “**less than $60 million**” (or the SRC revenue test applies), it becomes a non-accelerated filer.

**S7-2026-18** (issued 2026-05-19; comments were due 2026-07-20; last reviewed or updated 2026-07-31) remains **Proposed**. It would, among other things, raise the large-accelerated threshold from the current $700 million to $2 billion and collapse categories; do **not** teach $2 billion, a two-category model, or changed deadlines as current law.

Do not use fiscal year-end market cap as the 12b-2 measurement date. Do not ignore hysteresis (a large accelerated filer does not drop to accelerated at the first print under $700 million). Do not treat a brand-new IPO as immediately a large accelerated filer just because float exceeds $700 million (twelve months and one annual report are official conditions). SRC exists and can affect accelerated / large-accelerated eligibility through the listed revenue test; this lesson names that it exists and does not build a full SRC engine. No filer-status planning advice.

**Missing a 10-K or 10-Q statutory deadline is not automatically the end of the story: Rule 12b-25 requires a Form 12b-25 notification by the next business day, and the report can be deemed timely only if the filer meets the official conditions, including actually filing within 15 calendar days (annual) or 5 calendar days (quarterly) (checked 2026-11-12).** Do not rewrite the 60 / 75 / 90 and 40 / 45 deadline numbers, the 12b-2 thresholds, or the Form 10-S proposal sentence. Do not edit `examples/p2/09_filing_dates_toy.py` or `examples/p2/17_xbrl_pit_10b51_toy.py`. This section adds the official late-filing-notification rule, so a cleaner does not treat a missed statutory date as a hard “no filing exists,” or treat Form 12b-25 itself as the 10-K or 10-Q.

[17 CFR 240.12b-25(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-240.12b-25): if a listed report, including a Form 10-K or Form 10-Q, “is not filed within the time period prescribed for such report, the registrant, no later than one business day after the due date for such report, shall file a Form 12b-25,” with disclosure of its inability to file timely and the reasons in reasonable detail. 12b-25(b): the report “shall be deemed to be filed on the prescribed due date” only if the official conditions are met, including that the reason could not be eliminated without unreasonable effort or expense, that the annual report will be filed no later than the **fifteenth calendar day** following the prescribed due date or the quarterly report no later than the **fifth calendar day**, **and** the report is actually filed within that period. 12b-25(d): even then, the registrant is not eligible to use a 1933 Act registration form whose use is predicated on timely filed reports until the subject report is actually filed. EDGAR often labels the notification NT 10-K or NT 10-Q; the official form is Form 12b-25.

## SEC XBRL data: the `filed` date is your as-of date (checked 2026-11-11)

**A free, official source of fundamentals.** The SEC serves the financial-statement numbers that companies tag in XBRL (a machine-readable format inside 10-Q, 10-K, 8-K and similar filings) as JSON on `data.sec.gov`, with no API key ([SEC: EDGAR APIs](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-application-programming-interfaces)). `companyconcept` returns one company and one tag (for example `us-gaap/NetIncomeLoss`); `companyfacts` returns all tags for one company; `frames` returns one value per company for a calendar period. Fair access: no more than **10 requests per second** in total, and scripts should identify themselves; the SEC may block excessive or unidentified automated traffic ([SEC developer resources](https://www.sec.gov/about/developer-resources)).

**Every fact carries the filing that reported it.** Each record has `start`, `end`, `val`, `accn` (accession number), `fy`, `fp`, `form` and `filed`. The same period appears again in later filings, as comparatives in next year's 10-K, and in amendments. A real example ([Apple, NetIncomeLoss](https://data.sec.gov/api/xbrl/companyconcept/CIK0000320193/us-gaap/NetIncomeLoss.json), read 2026-11-11): for the fiscal year 2008-09-28 to 2009-09-26, the 10-K filed 2009-10-27 reported **5,704 million**; a 10-K/A filed 2010-01-25 reported **8,235 million** for the same year, and the 10-Ks of 2010 and 2011 repeat 8,235. Three traps:

1. **Pick by `filed`.** A decision on 2009-11-02 may only use 5,704 million. Taking the latest value is look-ahead.
2. **`frames` returns the last filed value.** The SEC says the frames API aggregates the fact "that is last filed" for each company and period, so its CY2009 value is 8,235. Fine for today's snapshot, wrong for a historical panel.
3. **`fy` and `fp` describe the filing, not the period.** The same FY2009 number appears with `fy=2010` and `fy=2011`. Identify periods by `start`/`end`.

**Backtest rule:** keep every record; for each decision date, take the latest record for that exact period with `filed` before the date (store `accn` so you can trace it); act no earlier than the next session after acceptance (see the EDGAR time rule above; `filed` is a date only, so the acceptance time comes from the filing index).

## Insider and large-holder filings: when are they "known"? (checked 2026-11-10)

Directors, officers and holders of more than 10% of a company's stock ("insiders") report their trades in that company's stock on Form 4 ([investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/forms-3-4-and-5)). The form is due before the end of the second business day after the date of execution; for certain planned transactions the clock starts on the deemed execution date under 16a-3(g)(4) ([17 CFR 240.16a-3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-240.16a-3)). So a Friday trade may not appear until Tuesday. An investor who goes above 5% generally files Schedule 13D within five business days and amends it within two business days after a material change. Qualifying institutions may file the shorter Schedule 13G instead, for example within 45 days after quarter-end; 13G deadlines differ by type of filer ([17 CFR 240.13d-1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-240.13d-1); [SEC 2023-219](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2023-219)).

In a backtest, a signal becomes usable once EDGAR has accepted the filing, not on the transaction date printed in the form. Using the transaction date usually looks up to two business days into the future; it can be longer for deemed execution dates (16a-3(g)(4)) or late filings. A filing accepted at 21:40 ET is on time for that day, but the regular session already closed at 16:00, so a daily backtest can act at the next session at the earliest.

One 2026 change: since 2026-03-18, directors and officers of foreign private issuers that have equity securities registered under Section 12 must also file Form 4, but the SEC has granted exemptive orders for several jurisdictions, including Canada, the European Economic Area, the UK and Australia ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/about/divisions-offices/division-corporation-finance/holding-foreign-insiders-accountable-act-section-16a-reporting-requirements)); the list may change, so read the SEC page rather than copying it. For these companies, "no Form 4 before 2026-03-18" does not mean insiders did not trade; they simply did not have to report. After that date, check the exemption list first.

**Rule 10b5-1 plans: a flag on Form 4 since 2023-04-01.** Insiders often sell under a pre-arranged trading plan adopted under Rule 10b5-1, which gives an affirmative defense against insider-trading liability if its conditions are met. Since the 2022 amendments, a director or officer cannot trade under a new plan until a **cooling-off period** ends: the later of **90 days** after adoption or **two business days** after the 10-Q/10-K for the fiscal quarter of adoption is disclosed, but never more than **120 days**; for other persons (not the company) it is **30 days** ([17 CFR 240.10b5-1(c)(1)(ii)(B)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-240.10b5-1)). Form 4 has a check box for transactions under a plan intended to satisfy Rule 10b5-1(c), with the plan's adoption date in the explanation ([Form 4, Instruction 10](https://www.sec.gov/files/form4.pdf)); it is required for Forms 4 and 5 filed on or after **2023-04-01** ([SEC press release 2022-222](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2022-222)). For data work: (1) the box lets you separate planned trades from others; (2) before 2023-04-01 the field does not exist, so "box not checked" in older filings means **unknown**, not "discretionary"; (3) a planned sale was decided at least the cooling-off period earlier. This is about labelling data correctly; this course gives no insider-trading signal. Lab: sections B–C of `17_xbrl_pit_10b51_toy.py` (output in the XBRL section above).

[Form 4, General Instruction 8](https://www.sec.gov/files/form4.pdf): “Use the code that most appropriately describes the transaction.”

- **General:** **P** — “Open market or private purchase of non-derivative or derivative security”; **S** — “Open market or private sale of non-derivative or derivative security.” **V** in the appropriate column marks a transaction “voluntarily reported earlier than required” (otherwise leave that column blank). - **Rule 16b-3:** **A** — “Grant, award or other acquisition pursuant to Rule 16b-3(d)”; **D** — “Disposition to the issuer of issuer equity securities pursuant to Rule 16b-3(e)”; **F** — “Payment of exercise price or tax liability by delivering or withholding securities incident to the receipt, exercise or vesting of a security issued in accordance with Rule 16b-3”; **I** — a discretionary transaction under 16b-3(f); **M** — “Exercise or conversion of derivative security exempted pursuant to Rule 16b-3.”

**Form 3 / Form 5 clocks.** [15 U.S.C. 78p(a)(2)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/78p) requires the initial statement “within 10 days after he or she becomes such beneficial owner, director, or officer, or within such shorter time as the Commission may establish by rule.” This lesson does **not** invent a shorter Commission deadline; none was found in [17 CFR 240.16a-3](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.16a-3). 16a-3(a) assigns that initial statement to Form 3, changes to Form 4, and annual statements to Form 5. [16a-3(f)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.16a-3): a Form 5 “shall be filed within 45 days after the issuer's fiscal year end” and reports specified holdings and transactions not previously reported; “no Form 5 shall be required where all transactions otherwise required to be reported on the Form 5 have been reported before the due date.” Those “10 days” and “45 days” are as written, not rewritten here as business days. **A Schedule 13G filer who loses 13G eligibility must switch to Schedule 13D within five business days; beneficial ownership is voting and/or investment power, not merely shares of record (checked 2026-11-12).** The initial 13D / 13G *filing lags* are already taught above and are not rewritten here. [17 CFR 240.13d-1(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.13d-1): a person that has reported more than 5% on Schedule 13G under paragraph (b) or (c), or is required to report the acquisition but has not yet filed, “shall immediately become subject to paragraph (a) … and shall file a statement on Schedule 13D … **within five business days** if, and shall remain subject to those requirements for so long as, the person: (i) Has acquired or holds the securities with a purpose or effect of changing or influencing control of the issuer, … and (ii) Is at that time the beneficial owner of more than five percent ….” From the time of that purpose or effect “until the expiration of the **tenth day from the date of the filing** of the Schedule 13D,” that person “shall not: (i) Vote or direct the voting of the securities described therein; or (ii) Acquire an additional beneficial ownership interest in any equity securities of the issuer …, nor of any person controlling the issuer.”

[13d-1(g)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.13d-1): a paragraph (b) 13G filer who “ceases to be a person specified in paragraph (b)(1)(ii)” or “determines that it no longer has acquired or holds the securities in the ordinary course of business” shall file a 13D or an amendment to 13G, as applicable, “**within five business days thereafter**,” if still above 5%. [13d-1(i)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.13d-1): “business day means any day, other than Saturday, Sunday, or a Federal holiday, from 12 a.m. to 11:59 p.m., Eastern Time.” In the lab, a paragraph (b) filer who loses eligibility on 2026-10-05 has a fifth business day of 2026-10-13, because 2026-10-12 is Columbus Day on that same OPM 2026 schedule.

[17 CFR 240.13d-3(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.13d-3): a beneficial owner “includes any person who, directly or indirectly, through any contract, arrangement, understanding, relationship, or otherwise has or shares: (1) Voting power … and/or, (2) Investment power ….” [13d-3(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.13d-3): all securities of the same class beneficially owned “shall be aggregated.” [13d-3(d)(1)(i)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.13d-3): a person “shall be deemed to be the beneficial owner of a security … if that person has the right to acquire beneficial ownership of such security … **within sixty days**,” including by option, warrant, right, or conversion; but if that right is acquired “with the purpose or effect of changing or influencing the control of the issuer,” the person is deemed the beneficial owner of the underlying “**immediately** upon such acquisition.” Securities “not outstanding which are subject to such options, warrants, rights or conversion privileges shall be deemed to be outstanding for the purpose of computing the percentage … owned by such person but shall not be deemed to be outstanding for the purpose of computing the percentage of the class by any other person.”

## Delisting returns and permanent IDs (CRSP CIZ) (checked 2026-11-10)

**A delisting is not "end of data"; it is a return you must book.** After a stock delists, your final outcome is not necessarily the last trade price. It may trade over the counter, pay a liquidation or merger consideration, or go to zero. CRSP (the Center for Research in Security Prices; part of [Morningstar](https://newsroom.morningstar.com/news/news-details/2026/Morningstar-Completes-Acquisition-of-CRSP-and-Extends-Relationship-with-Vanguard/default.aspx) since 2026-02-02) calls the return from the last trade price to the post-delisting value the delisting return. Per the CRSP CIZ guide (July 2026 edition; product page: [CRSP US stock databases](https://www.crsp.org/research/crsp-us-stock-databases/)), the monthly return MthRet already includes delisting returns where appropriate, so do not apply them a second time. It excludes them in two flagged cases: the post-delisting value comes more than 10 trading days after delisting (flag G), or the delisting return is missing (flag M). You must decide how to treat those two cases and document the choice. Shumway (1997, *Journal of Finance*) found that most stocks delisted for bankruptcy and other negative reasons lack a correct delisting return, and that the omitted returns are large ([doi:10.1111/j.1540-6261.1997.tb03818.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1997.tb03818.x)). Dropping those stocks, or treating missing values as zero, makes a backtest look too good. Also, tickers change and get reused by other companies. Key your data on a permanent ID such as PERMNO (CRSP's permanent security identifier) or CIK (the SEC's company identifier), and store the ticker as a time-varying attribute.

## Reverse splits and Nasdaq's $1 bid-price rule: listing status as data (checked 2026-11-11)

**Why a data lesson cares.** A stock that drifts below $1 on Nasdaq starts a listing clock. Companies often answer with a **reverse split** (for example 10 old shares become 1 new share, so the price is 10 times higher). In a raw price series that shows up as a huge fake jump, and the stock may still be delisted later. Both the split and the listing status need to be in your data.

**The rules, in order (Nasdaq Rule 5810(c)(3)(A), as described in SEC orders):**

1. **Failure:** the closing bid is below $1.00 for **30 consecutive business days**. The company then gets a compliance period of **180 calendar days** from Nasdaq's notice; some Nasdaq Capital Market companies can get a second 180 days. It regains compliance with a closing bid of $1.00 or more for at least **10 consecutive business days** ([SEC 34-102245](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nasdaq/2025/34-102245.pdf), 2025-01-17).
2. **Recent reverse splits:** if the company effected a reverse split over the prior one-year period, or effected one or more reverse splits over the prior two-year period with a cumulative ratio of **250 shares or more to one**, it gets **no** compliance period and Nasdaq issues a delisting determination (Rule 5810(c)(3)(A)(iv), [SEC 34-100767 Exhibit 5](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nasdaq/2024/34-100767-ex5.pdf); see also 34-102245). A company can still appeal to a Hearings Panel.
3. **$0.10 rule:** a closing bid of $0.10 or less for ten consecutive business days leads to a delisting determination and **suspension** of trading on Nasdaq, with no compliance period. The SEC approved this on 2025-12-05, and it became operative 45 days later ([SEC 34-104318, 90 FR 57225](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/12/10/2025-22398/self-regulatory-organizations-the-nasdaq-stock-market-llc-notice-of-filing-of-amendment-no-1-and)). As that order describes it, under the prior version the $0.10 provision applied only once a company had already failed the $1 rule and was in a compliance period (it cut the remaining period short); the new version no longer depends on a compliance period, and Nasdaq issues the delisting determination and suspends trading as soon as it is triggered. The company can still appeal to a Hearings Panel, which may grant an exception of up to 180 days from the date of the Staff Delisting Determination to regain compliance, but the stock does not trade on Nasdaq while the appeal is pending.
4. **A rule that is approved but not in force:** a new $5 million Market Value of Listed Securities requirement was approved on 2026-07-22 ([SEC 34-105971](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nasdaq/2026/34-105971.pdf)), then automatically stayed on 2026-07-29, when notices of intention to petition for review were filed (SEC Rule of Practice 431(e)). On 2026-09-11 the Commission granted review and kept the approval order stayed "pending further order" ([SEC 34-106338](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/other/2026/34-106338.pdf)). Nasdaq filed on 2026-09-25, and the SEC noticed it on 2026-10-05 in 34-106594, moving the effective date to after the stay is lifted ([SEC 34-106594](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nasdaq/2026/34-106594.pdf)). Do not apply it in a backtest until the SEC lifts the stay.

**Backtest rules:** adjust the split day with the split ratio (see the corporate-actions section above); keep a `listing_status` column with the dates of the deficiency notice, delisting determination and suspension; when a stock is suspended and moves to the over-the-counter market, book the delisting return (see the CIZ section) instead of dropping it. These are listing rules, not trading signals; this lesson does not claim that reverse splits predict returns. NYSE has its own, different price rules; see the next section.

## NYSE's price rules are different: a 30-day average, 200:1, and a $0.25 floor from 2027-07-01 (checked 2026-11-11)

**Same idea, different arithmetic.** NYSE Listed Company Manual Section 802.01C, as restated in [SEC 34-102201](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nyse/2025/34-102201.pdf) (2025-01-15):

1. **Failure:** the **average** closing price over a consecutive **30 trading-day** period is below $1.00. Nasdaq counts 30 consecutive days with a closing bid below $1; NYSE averages. A stock that alternates $0.97 / $1.01 fails NYSE's test but never builds a 30-day run.
2. **Cure:** six months from NYSE's notice. The company regains compliance if, on the **last trading day of any calendar month** in that period, both its closing price and its 30-trading-day average closing price are at least $1.00. One good close is not enough.
3. **Recent reverse splits (approved 2025-01-15):** if the company effected a reverse split over the prior one-year period, or reverse splits with a cumulative ratio of **200 shares or more to one** over the prior two years, it gets **no** cure period and NYSE immediately starts suspension and delisting procedures (Nasdaq's cumulative threshold is 250:1).
4. **$0.25 Minimum Trading Price (approved, not yet effective):** a closing price below **$0.25 on any trading day** means immediate suspension and delisting proceedings, with no cure period. ## Issuer repurchases: the Rule 10b-18 safe harbor, and when you can see the volume (checked 2026-11-11)

When a company buys back its own shares in the open market (a share repurchase, or buyback), the share count changes, and volume and prices may change too. Treat this as corporate-action data, not as a trading signal.

**1. Rule 10b-18 is a voluntary safe harbor, not a required way to buy.** [17 CFR 240.10b-18](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-240.10b-18) gives an issuer (and its affiliated purchasers) a safe harbor from manipulation liability when it buys its own common stock in the open market. If the conditions are met, the purchases are not deemed to violate Exchange Act Sections 9(a)(2) or 10(b), or Rule 10b-5, solely because of their manner, timing, price or volume. Compliance is **voluntary**; falling outside the conditions is not itself a presumption of manipulation (paragraph (d)). But to enter the safe harbor, **that day's Rule 10b-18 purchases must satisfy all four conditions**; fail one, and that day's purchases are all outside the harbor ([SEC staff FAQ](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/staff-guidance/trading-markets-frequently-asked-questions/division-trading-markets-answers-frequently-asked-questions-concerning-rule-10b-18-safe-harbor)). The four conditions, in plain language:

- **Broker:** on any single day, the purchases must go through only one broker or dealer (unsolicited purchases, and a different broker after the close, have exceptions).
- **Timing:** the purchase must not be the opening (regular way) purchase reported in the consolidated system; it also must not be effected in a window before the scheduled close: the last **10 minutes** if the security's ADTV value is **$1 million** or more and its public float value is **$150 million** or more; otherwise the last **30 minutes**. The window is measured from the **scheduled** close, so on a 1:00 p.m. early-close day the 10-minute window starts at 12:50 p.m.
- **Price:** the purchase price may not exceed the higher of the highest independent bid and the last independent transaction price then quoted or reported in the consolidated system.
- **Volume:** the day's total is generally capped at **25% of ADTV**. ADTV is the average daily trading volume reported in the four calendar weeks preceding the week of the purchase. Once a week the issuer may instead effect one block purchase, with no other Rule 10b-18 purchases that day; that block need not fit under the 25% cap, but it is left out of later ADTV calculations.

The safe harbor does **not** cover repurchases made while the issuer has material nonpublic information, and it does not cover privately negotiated off-market trades. This lesson does not discuss whether a company should repurchase shares.

**2. The official repurchase figures you see are usually monthly totals, and only when the 10-Q or 10-K is filed.** Current disclosure is [Item 703](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-229.703) of Regulation S-K: in the 10-Q or 10-K the company reports, by month, shares purchased, the average price, shares bought under publicly announced plans, and how much of the authorization remains. A board "authorization" press release is a cap, not executed daily volume. In 2023 the SEC adopted a rule that would have required daily repurchase disclosure (including a new Form SR), but the Fifth Circuit **vacated** that rule on **2023-12-19**, and disclosure reverted to Item 703 ([SEC announcement, 2024-02-09](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/whats-new/further-announcement-regarding-share-repurchase-disclosure-modernization-rule); technical amendments at 89 FR 24372, effective 2024-04-08). Backtest rule: do not treat an authorization announcement as that day's buyback volume; use an Item 703 monthly total only after the corresponding 10-Q or 10-K is filed and public.

**3. After the primary session closes, a limited safe harbor continues until last-sale reporting ends.** [17 CFR 240.10b-18(b)(2)(iv)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-240.10b-18) already allows Rule 10b-18 purchases “following the close of the primary trading session until the termination of the period in which last sale prices are reported in the consolidated system,” so long as all of these are true:

- the purchase is **not** “the opening transaction of the session following the close of the primary trading session”;
- the price does “**not exceed the lower of** the closing price of the primary trading session in the **principal market** for the security **and** any lower bids or sale prices subsequently reported in the consolidated system”;
- the other conditions of the section (including the day’s volume limit) are met.

The issuer “may use one broker or dealer … during this period that may be different from the broker or dealer that it used during the primary trading session.” The [SEC 10b-18 FAQ](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/staff-guidance/trading-markets-frequently-asked-questions/division-trading-markets-answers-frequently-asked-questions-concerning-rule-10b-18-safe-harbor) (Timing Condition) restates the same limited after-hours safe harbor. That FAQ was last reviewed or updated **December 2, 2016**. Neither it nor the NYSE Extended-Hours Trading FAQ (August 19, 2026) maps this window onto **21:00–04:00 overnight / 23/5**. This lesson does **not** write that overnight 23/5 buybacks are inside the safe harbor, and it does **not** write that they are outside it: official 10b-18 sources have not mapped those hours. The four regular-hours conditions and Item 703 stay as already taught.

## Spin-offs and mergers: the fake crash and the sudden disappearance (checked 2026-11-10)

In a spin-off, a parent company distributes shares of a subsidiary to its own shareholders, usually pro rata, and the subsidiary becomes a separate company ([investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/spin-offs)). Once the new company trades, the parent's share price is adjusted lower, because its value no longer includes the spun-off unit ([FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/corporate-spinoffs)).

If you compute returns from raw closes, the ex-distribution day shows a fake crash. In the lab below the parent falls from 100 to 81, a raw return of −19%. But each parent share also received 0.5 child shares that closed at 40, so the holder's actual return is +1%. CRSP's calculations guide treats spin-offs like splits and stock dividends: they are "split events" handled with a price adjustment factor on the ex-distribution date. Shares and volume are adjusted only for splits and stock dividends, not for spin-offs. The newly received security is linked back to the parent through DisPERMNO in the distribution records.

Mergers need the same care: an acquired stock delists, and its final return comes from the merger consideration, so do not drop it as "end of data" (read this with the delisting section above). Timing matters too. The issuer reports the effect on shareholders' cost basis on IRS Form 8937, due by the 45th day after the action or January 15 of the next year, whichever is earlier ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8937)). Those official numbers can therefore appear more than a month after the ex-date. This lesson covers data and timing only, not tax treatment.

## Tender offers: announcement is not completion (checked 2026-11-11)

A tender offer is a public invitation to shareholders to sell their shares, usually at a stated price, during a stated window ([investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/tender-offer)). In a backtest, the announcement day is not the day every share converts. Until the offer expires or is completed, the target still trades, and not every holder tenders.

[Rule 14e-1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/section-240.14e-1) sets a minimum timetable. The offer must stay open at least **20 business days** from the date it is first published, sent or given to security holders (14e-1(a)). If the bidder later changes the percentage of the class sought, the consideration offered, or the dealer's soliciting fee, the offer must remain open at least **10 business days** from the date that change is first published, sent or given (14e-1(b)). If the offer is extended, a notice of the extension must be issued by press release or other public announcement; that notice must include disclosure of the approximate number of securities deposited to date; and it must be issued no later than the **earlier of** (i) 9:00 a.m. Eastern time on the next business day after the scheduled expiration date of the offer, or (ii) if the class is registered on one or more national securities exchanges, the first opening of any one of those exchanges on the next business day after the scheduled expiration date (14e-1(d)). "Business day" is defined in the tender-offer rules (14d-1(g)(3) and 13e-4(a)(3)); the lab below only counts Monday–Friday and ignores holidays.

On **2026-04-16**, the SEC issued an exemptive order that lets **some** offers for equity securities stay open for **10 business days** instead of 20, but only when every condition in that order is met (for example, a cash offer at a fixed price, with further conditions that differ for third-party offers under Regulation 14D and issuer offers under Rule 13e-4). The order is **not** a repeal of the 20-day rule. If a competing offer appears, the shortened timetable can fall away and the 20-day rule can apply again. Do not code "US tender offers now last 10 days."

## Dividend dates and the yfinance auto_adjust trap (checked 2026-11-10)

**1. Four dates of a cash dividend.** A company announces a dividend (declaration date), fixes who is on its books (record date), the stock starts trading without the dividend (ex-dividend date), and the cash is paid (payment date). What decides whether you get the dividend is the **ex-dividend date**: if you buy on the ex-date or later, you do not get this payment. Since US settlement moved to T+1, investor.gov says the ex-date is **usually the record date**, or one business day before it if the record date is not a business day ([investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/ex-dividend-dates-when-are-you-entitled-stock-and)). Its example: declared 2026-03-02, record 2026-03-16, ex-date 2026-03-16, paid 2026-03-17.

- **Exception:** if a cash dividend, stock dividend or split is 25% or more of the stock's value, the ex-date is deferred to one business day **after** the payment date ([FINRA Rule 11140(b)](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/11140)); someone who sells between the record date and that ex-date delivers a "due bill" for the distribution ([FINRA Rule 11630](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/11630)). For ADRs and foreign securities, FINRA designates the ex-date separately (11140(b)(3)). A stock dividend below 25% follows the normal rule: ex-date equals the record date.
- **Backtest bookkeeping:** book the total return on the **ex-date** (the price drops that day) but receive the cash on the **payment date**. Keep them separate, and never count the dividend twice. The double-count lab in section D of `05_data_versions_calendar.py` is the same idea one level down.
- **Tax character (a pointer only; no hour shift here):** a cash distribution is not always a dividend. A nondividend distribution / return of capital in Form 1099-DIV box 3 reduces basis and is not itself a dividend; see `compliance-ethics`. The FINRA 11140(b) 25%+ ex-date deferral is a *when-the-price-drops* rule, not a tax-character rule.
- **Advanced:** vendor `ex_date` fields can be missing or filled with the payment date. Check that ex-date is not after the payment date, except for cash dividends, stock dividends or splits of 25% or more.

**Rule 10b-17: the issuer must generally give notice no later than 10 days before the record date (checked 2026-11-12).** Official close, T+1 ex-date mechanics, and the FINRA 11140(b) 25%+ deferral are already taught above. [17 CFR 240.10b-17](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.10b-17) makes it a “manipulative or deceptive device or contrivance” for any issuer of a class of securities publicly traded by the use of any means or instrumentality of interstate commerce or of the mails or of any facility of any national securities exchange “to fail to give notice in accordance with paragraph (b) of this section” of: (1) a dividend or other distribution in cash or in kind, except an ordinary interest payment on a debt security, but including a dividend or distribution of any security of the same or another issuer; (2) a stock split or reverse split; or (3) a rights or other subscription offering.

Notice is deemed given only if given to “the National Association of Securities Dealers, Inc., **no later than 10 days prior to the record date** involved,” or, “in case of a rights subscription or other offering if such 10 days advance notice is not practical, on or before the record date and in no event later than the effective date of the registration statement to which the offering relates,” **or** given “in accordance with procedures of the national securities exchange or exchanges upon which a security of such issuer is registered pursuant to section 12 of the Act which contain requirements substantially comparable to those set forth in paragraph (b)(1).” The Commission may exempt an issuer. The rule “shall not apply … to redeemable securities issued by open-end investment companies and unit investment trusts registered with the Commission under the Investment Company Act of 1940.”

That “10 days” is **not** “10 business days”; the rule text does not say business days. Do not invent an automatic ex-date delay under 10b-17 itself (the 25%+ deferral is already taught under FINRA 11140(b)). A late or missing 10b-17 notice is a **data-quality / timing flag** around the record date, the ex-date, and the declared amount — not proof the dividend did not happen. The rule text still names the NASD; this lesson does not invent a FINRA-substituted addressee. No issuer or product recommendation.

**2. Version trap: yfinance changed what `Close` means.** yfinance 0.2.51 (released 2024-12-19) changed `yf.download()` so that `auto_adjust` defaults to `True`, matching `Ticker.history()`; 0.2.53 (2025-02-15) started warning when you do not pass it ([yfinance CHANGELOG](https://github.com/ranaroussi/yfinance/blob/main/CHANGELOG.rst)). The official docs describe the two shapes ([yfinance price adjustment](https://ranaroussi.github.io/yfinance/advanced/price_adjustment.html)):

| Call | What `Close` is | `Adj Close` column |
|---|---|---|
| `auto_adjust=True` (default since 0.2.51) | adjusted for splits **and** dividends | none |
| `auto_adjust=False` | Yahoo's price as returned, **already split-adjusted** | yes, also dividend-adjusted |

- So the same column name `Close` changes meaning across versions, and Yahoo does not give you a fully unadjusted price. That differs from this lesson's rule of using raw prices for the share and cash ledger.
- Engineering rules: pin the version, always write `auto_adjust` explicitly, check the column names, and record the field meaning in your data sheet. The current release, 1.8.0 (2026-11-03), added a price-repair fix for split-like jumps without a matching split; repair changes the data, so log whether it was on.
- yfinance remains an unofficial wrapper for personal use (see the source table above); the course does not download by default.

## Common mistakes / pitfalls

1. Backtesting a decade with today’s members only.  
2. Fundamentals without PIT.  
2b. Crypto archives that look complete but fail availability-time audits (same-bar / funding lag / backfill / unverified OI).  
3. UTC vs local off-by-one.  
4. Mixing raw/adj silently.  
5. No hash / no version.
6. Treating info bars as an HFT execution recipe rather than a research sampling clock.
7. Comparing clocks on different underlying tapes without saying so.
8. In a toy setup, first rolling a mean over limit-up/limit-down or halt closes marked non-executable and then dropping those rows (upstream contamination); or, conversely, treating "could not place an order at the time" as "the information was unavailable".
