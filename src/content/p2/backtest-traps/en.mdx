- **Survivorship:** today’s listed names ≠ the historical investable universe — replaying only survivors inflates means (numeric demo lives in data-cleaning).

## Index membership as of each date (checked 2026-11-10)

**Today's index members are survivors too.** If your universe is "members of index X", each historical date must use the membership list as it stood then. Today's members were selected by past price moves, so replaying history with them is survivorship bias. An index change has three key dates: the rank day (whose data decides who enters and leaves), the announcement day, and the effective day. Using the new list before it is announced is look-ahead. A current example: the Russell US indexes, previously reconstituted once a year in June, moved to twice a year, June and December, starting in 2026 ([FTSE Russell](https://www.lseg.com/en/media-centre/press-releases/ftse-russell/2026/russell-reconstitution-2026-schedule)). For December 2026, rank day was October 30. Preliminary additions and deletions come out after the US close on November 13, the changes take effect after the close on December 11, and markets open on December 14 with the new membership ([FTSE Russell](https://www.lseg.com/en/media-centre/press-releases/ftse-russell/2026/ftse-russell-announces-december-2026-russell-us-indexes-reconstitution-schedule)). A backtest decision dated November 1, 2026 cannot use that list. If your backtest spans 2026, update the universe on the new schedule; do not hard-code "every June".

## Nasdaq-100: rules changed on 2026-05-01, and the December 2026 reconstitution (checked 2026-11-11)

**The rules of an index can change too, not just its members.** Nasdaq updated the Nasdaq-100 (NDX) methodology with effect from 2026-05-01 ([methodology](https://indexes.nasdaqomx.com/docs/Methodology_NDX.pdf); [consultation conclusion, February 2026](https://indexes.nasdaqomx.com/docs/Nasdaq-100_Index_Consultation_February_2026_Summary_of_Responses_and_Conclusion.pdf); [methodology-change FAQ](https://indexes.nasdaqomx.com/docs/2026_NDX_Changes_FAQ.pdf)). The points that matter for a backtest, all from those three documents:

- **Ranking uses full market cap.** Companies are ranked by Full Market Capitalization. For directly listed (non-ADR) shares and Primary ADRs, this counts listed **and unlisted** shares (for example an unlisted share class held by founders). An ADR (American Depositary Receipt) is a US-listed certificate that stands for shares of a foreign company; a Primary ADR is one whose underlying shares are not listed or traded anywhere else. For a Non-Primary ADR (the underlying shares have their main listing on a foreign market), only the listed depositary shares count, not the foreign-listed or unlisted shares. Weights still use only eligible listed shares.
- **Low-float stocks get capped weights.** Weights use Modified Market Capitalization: each security's total shares outstanding (TSO) is capped at **3 times** its free-floating shares. The consultation proposed 5 times; the conclusion chose 3. So a stock with more than 33.3% float is weighted at its full listed value, and a stock with 10% float at 30% of it. The old **10% minimum-float** entry requirement (in place from June 2024) was removed. Float is updated at each quarterly rebalance reference date.
- **Quarterly removal by rank.** At the March, June and September rebalances, members ranked outside the **top 125** by Full Market Capitalization are removed, and replaced if the count drops below 100. This replaces the old "10 basis point rule". The FAQ says the 10 basis point rule and intra-quarter TSO updates ended on 2026-05-01, while the top-125 review and ranking on total (listed plus unlisted) shares first applied at the quarterly rebalance effective 2026-06-22. The 3x low-float weight cap applies to existing members from that 2026-06-22 rebalance, but a stock added through Fast Entry from 2026-05-01 gets the cap immediately.
- **Fast Entry.** A company that is not a member can be added early if its Full Market Capitalization ranks within the **top 40** current members. No member is removed to make room, so the count can temporarily exceed 100. For an IPO: it is ranked as of the close of its **7th** trading day, the addition is announced after the close of its **10th** trading day, and it is typically added after **15** trading days. If the 15th trading day falls in the same month as a scheduled reconstitution or rebalance, it joins as part of that event when its 7th trading day is on or before the event's reference date; otherwise it joins no sooner than five trading days after the event's effective date. It must have an average daily value traded of at least $5 million from its first trading day. Without Fast Entry, a security normally needs three full calendar months of trading on an eligible exchange, not counting the listing month.
- **Annual reconstitution calendar.** Reference date: last trading day of November. Announcement: after the close on the sixth trading day before the effective date. Effective: market open on the first trading day after the third Friday of December. To be considered in December, a security normally must have listed by the last trading day of August; Fast Entry is the exception.

**The 2026 dates, derived from those rules (not announced dates).** November 30, 2026 is a Monday and a trading day (the market is closed on Thanksgiving, November 26), so the reference date is **2026-11-30**. The third Friday of December is December 18, so the effective date is the open on **Monday 2026-12-21**. There is no market holiday between December 11 and December 21, so counting six trading days back gives an announcement after the close on **2026-12-11**. These are derived here, not announced; the announcement on Nasdaq's Global Index Watch site is authoritative, so check it before using any date.

**Backtest rule:** for an NDX universe, store point-in-time membership with announcement and effective dates, and store which methodology version was in force on each date (before 2026-05-01: 10% minimum float and the 10 basis point rule; after it: Fast Entry, the 3x float cap and the top-125 review; the top-125 review, total-share ranking and the 3x cap for existing members first applied on 2026-06-22, while Fast Entry additions from 2026-05-01 get the 3x cap at once). Do not assume a new listing needs months before it can join: under Fast Entry, a large IPO can join after about 15 trading days. A decision made before the announcement cannot use the new list. This section makes no prediction about which companies will be added or removed, recommends no fund that tracks the index, and makes no claim about price moves around inclusion.

## S&P 500: chosen by a committee, announced at 17:15 ET (checked 2026-11-11)

**The rules say who may be added, not who will be.** Unlike Russell or the Nasdaq-100, the S&P 500 has no mechanical ranking. Per the [S&P U.S. Indices Methodology](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/methodologies/methodology-sp-us-indices.pdf) (November 2026 edition), an Index Committee selects members from eligible companies, keeps the count fixed at 500 and also considers sector balance. So you cannot rebuild the index from its rules; use a historical membership list with dates. The rules are still useful to see who could be a candidate, and they contain several date traps.

- **Addition criteria (current edition):** US domicile and US periodic reports (10-K, 10-Q, 8-K); a listing on an eligible US exchange (the list includes NYSE, the Nasdaq tiers, Cboe exchanges and the Texas Stock Exchange; OTC is excluded); total company market cap of at least **$22.7 billion**; float-adjusted market cap of at least half of that; investable weight factor (the float share, IWF) of at least 0.10; float-adjusted liquidity ratio of at least 0.75 and at least 250,000 shares traded in each of the six months before the evaluation date; **positive GAAP net income for the most recent quarter and for the sum of the last four quarters**; and, for IPOs and former SPACs, 12 months of trading. ADRs, ETFs, BDCs, LPs and similar structures are ineligible.
- **The thresholds move.** Appendix A lists the minimum market cap by date: $18.0 billion after the close of 2024-04-01, $20.5 billion after the close of 2025-01-02, $22.7 billion after the close of 2025-07-01. A screen for 2025-06 that uses $22.7 billion is applying a later rule.
- **Evaluation date:** the open of trading two business days before the announcement. The earnings test must use only results already filed by then (see the XBRL `filed` date in `data-cleaning`).
- **Addition, not continued membership:** a member that later breaks a criterion is not removed automatically. A removed company must wait at least one year before it is screened again.
- **Timing:** no scheduled reconstitution; changes happen as needed, are announced with at least three business days' notice (less at the Committee's discretion), and announcements come at **05:15 PM Eastern**, after the close.

**Backtest rule:** store each change with its announcement timestamp and effective date. A daily backtest can react no earlier than the next session's open. This section makes no claim about price moves around additions, predicts no additions, and recommends no index fund.

## IPOs: the offering price is not your fill (checked 2026-11-10)

An IPO's offering price is negotiated between the company and its underwriters, based on market conditions, valuation and investor demand. The SEC's investor bulletin notes that underwriters often allocate most IPO shares to institutional and high-net-worth clients, and that individuals more commonly buy once the shares trade in the public market. It also warns that the offering price may bear little relationship to the trading price ([SEC investor bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins-17)). So "buy at the IPO price" is usually not an executable assumption. In the lab, the offering price is 20, the first open is 26 and the close is 24. Measured from the offering price, day one returns +20%; bought at the open, it returns −7.69%.

A newly public company typically has no prior reporting history and only a short price history. A signal that needs 60 trading days should output "no signal" until 60 days exist; do not quietly shorten the window. Filter the universe by listing date and document the rule. Insiders also usually sign lock-up agreements, and most prevent sales for 180 days; the terms are in the prospectus ([investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/initial-public-offerings-lockup-agreements)). When a lock-up ends, many shares may become available for sale. Take the end date from the prospectus and company announcements; do not assume "IPO date + 180 days" for every stock.

## Walk-forward lab

Rolling windows pick the best MA length on each **train** block, then score the next **test** block without refitting inside the test.

Lab summary (10 folds): mean train SR≈**0.25**, mean test SR≈**−0.89**, median test≈**−1.75**, fraction of test SR>0 ≈**0.30**. Per-fold table is in the run output below.

## Why some walk-forward test Sharpes are NaN

Folds **6, 7, 9** print `sharpe_test=NaN` because the test window’s position path is **flat** (PnL≈0 → sample std=0), so Sharpe is undefined. The summary **mean/median skip those NaN folds** (pandas default); `frac_test_positive` treats NaN as not >0.

## False hits under the null (context for mean_false_hits≈2.78)

With n=**299** returns, under a Normal null the SE of the **annualized** Sharpe is ≈ √(252/299) ≈ **0.918**. Then P(|SR|>1.5) ≈ **0.102**, so among **30** trials the expected hit count is ≈ **3.07**. The lab’s Monte Carlo mean_false_hits≈**2.78** (50 seeds) sits close to that; the small gap is expected from ±1 discrete signs vs Gaussian noise.

## Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR)

From Bailey & López de Prado (2014), *The Deflated Sharpe Ratio*: DSR is a **PSR whose threshold is the expected maximum SR under multiple testing**. Under a null with mean 0 and cross-trial σ_SR:


```text
E[\max SR]\approx \sigma_{SR}\Big[(1-\gamma)\Phi^{-1}(1-\tfrac1N)+\gamma\,\Phi^{-1}(1-\tfrac1{Ne})\Big]
```


with Euler–Mascheroni γ≈0.5772. Then `mathrm{DSR}=widehat{PSR}(SR^*=E[max])`. As **N** grows, E[max] rises and **DSR falls** — even for a fixed selected trial.

Lab (selected train-best w=35): PSR(0)≈**0.42**; DSR drops from ≈**0.42** (N=1) toward ≈**0.06** (N=200). Chart: `/p2/dsr_vs_N.png`.

## Combinatorial purged CV (CPCV) teaching sketch

**Intuition:** A single train/hold-out split gives you **one** out-of-sample Sharpe. If that window is lucky or unlucky, you may love or hate a rule for the wrong reason. Combinatorial purged cross-validation (AFML ch.12) cuts the timeline into \(N\) contiguous groups, takes every combination of \(k\) groups as the test block, purges train labels that overlap those tests, and rebuilds several backtest **paths**. You then read the **distribution** of path metrics.

**Counts:** combinations \(C(N,k)\); paths \(\varphi[N,k]=C(N-1,k-1)\). Lab: \(N=6\), \(k=2\) → \(C(6,2)=15\) combinations and \(\varphi=C(5,1)=5\) paths.

**Numeric toy** (honest MA pnl): combo test Sharpes mean ≈ **−0.82**, std ≈ **1.44**; a single 2/3–1/3 hold-out yields **one** OOS SR ≈ **−1.34**. The cloud is the point. Paths **reuse** test groups — they are **not** independent DSR trials.

![CPCV SR distribution](/p2/cpcv_sr_dist.png)

**Disclaimer:** this demo is a **teaching sketch**, not production AFML `CombinatorialPurgedKFold`. The same combinatorial spirit reappears when `ml-leakage-control` scores many purged folds.

## One-switch decision-time leakage (TEMP_CENTER / EXEC_OPEN)

Chronological train/test splits can still be wrong. [When Alpha Disappears](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23959) (Zhang et al.) builds a **one-switch** benchmark: hold the panel, walk-forward split, model family, horizon, portfolio rule, and cost convention fixed — then flip **one** protocol rule and measure **Leakage Gain** = metric(leaky) − metric(CLEAN).

**Intuition:** “Out-of-sample” only means the *calendar* is later. It does **not** mean the *information set* at decision time was legal.

Two switches dominate their tables:

| Switch | What breaks | Clean alternative |
|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **TEMP_CENTER** | Centered rolling features use future observations around \(t\) | Trailing rolling only |
| **EXEC_OPEN** | Signal uses same-day post-open fields (H/L/C/V) while assuming a same-day **open** fill | Decide after close \(t\); enter at open \(t+1\) |

Lab toy (diagnostic inflation, **not** deployable alpha):

```text
CLEAN SR@toy≈-0.741
TEMP_CENTER SR≈6.265  LeakageGain_SR≈+7.006
EXEC_OPEN   SR≈20.816  LeakageGain_SR≈+21.556
```

Large positive Leakage Gain means the **protocol** made the future easier to see — not that the model suddenly became a genius. Paper also finds NORM_GLOBAL / STRUCT_GRAPH / EXEC_CLOSE often weak. Short reuse note lives in `ml-leakage-control`.




## FOMC: macro events have exact release times too (checked 2026-11-10)

The Federal Reserve's Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) holds eight scheduled meetings a year, plus others as needed, and usually publishes the minutes three weeks after the decision; around holidays they may come earlier (e.g. 2024-11-26, 2025-12-30); use the calendar page as the authority ([Fed calendar](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm)). Each policy statement has an exact release time. The 28 October 2026 statement, for example, says "For release at 2:00 p.m. EDT" ([statement](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20261028a.htm)), which is 02:00 on 29 October in Beijing. Regular US trading runs 09:30–16:00 Eastern, so the news lands in the middle of the session.

In a daily backtest, "trading" that statement at the same day's open is look-ahead, the same decision-time leak as EXEC_OPEN above. The earliest fill is after 14:00, which on daily bars usually means that day's close or the next open, and that close already contains part of the market's reaction. Minutes work the same way: a feature built from the minutes becomes usable on the release date, not the meeting date. 14:00 is the time printed on this one statement; read each statement page for its own time.

## Earnings releases: the event time is the release, not the quarter end or the 8-K date (checked 2026-11-11)

**Three different dates.** A quarter ends; weeks later the company publishes an earnings release; it then furnishes a Form 8-K under **Item 2.02**, which must give the date of the announcement and attach the release as an exhibit ([Form 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/files/form8-k.pdf)). Unless another deadline applies, an 8-K is due within **four business days** of the event (General Instruction B.1). So the 8-K date can be later than the release.

**Regulation FD.** If a company intentionally discloses material nonpublic information to market professionals or to holders who may trade on it, it must make the information public **at the same time**; for an unintentional disclosure, "promptly": as soon as reasonably practicable, and no later than 24 hours or the start of the next day's NYSE trading, whichever is later, after a senior official learns of it ([17 CFR 243.100–101](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/part-243)). For a backtest, the public release is when the news becomes available to everyone.

**Time of day decides day 0.** Regular trading runs 09:30–16:00 ET. A release at 07:00 lands before the open, so that day's open already reflects it. A release at 12:30 lands mid-session: day 0 is that day, but its open came before the news, so filling at that open is look-ahead (like EXEC_OPEN above). A release at 16:05 lands after the close, so day 0 is the next session; a Friday evening release maps to Monday.

**Backtest rule:** store the release timestamp with its time zone. If you only have a date, assume it came after the close and start at the next session. If you only have the 8-K, its EDGAR acceptance time never looks ahead, because the filing is public once accepted, but it may be later than the release. Never date an earnings feature to the quarter end. This lesson teaches timing only, not an earnings strategy.

## PBO / CSCV + evaporation curve — DSR ≠ leakage

**Intuition:** Deflated Sharpe and PBO ask: *among many honest backtests, how often is the in-sample winner a fluke?* They do **not** ask: *was the information set contaminated?* [What survives honest evaluation?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27734) (Gençay) makes both corrections **structural**: a registry that cannot express look-ahead, plus a **complete trial ledger** that indexes DSR/PBO to the agent’s own search. Classic PBO/CSCV: [Bailey et al. PDF](https://davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/backtest-prob.pdf).

**Toy (lab):** six synthetic strategies on a 3-year random walk — honest MA windows, a noise arm, and a planted **future-return oracle** (`pnl = |r_{t+1}|`).

```text

best_honest ma_20 design_SR≈1.05 held_SR≈1.12
evaporation: as k grows, deflation threshold rises (k=5 thr≈0.70) while best IS SR plateaus ≈1.05
CSCV_sketch S=4 combos=6 strategies=5 PBO_hat≈0.67  (teaching sketch)
```

![Evaporation curve sketch](/p2/pbo_evaporation.png)

**Punchline:** Gençay’s E1 planted oracle still posts DSR≈1 at Sharpe ~35 — **DSR corrects selection among honest backtests; leakage needs a structural guardrail.** Tiny CSCV here is **not** production `CombinatorialPurgedKFold`. Agent-search ledger note continues in `frontier-llm-agents`.

## Admission gates / injected-truth — precision ≠ higher returns

**Intuition:** Automated research inserts **admission gates** (must-pass criteria) before adopting a strategy. [Zheng (arXiv:2610.07701)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07701) tests gates with an **injected-truth** protocol plus a **random-admission control** at the same adoption rate — only then can you tell “informative” from “merely stricter.”

**Boundary (paper):** Gates beat random-admission for weak train **t ≲ 2.5**, at the cost of adoption ~**1–7%**. For strong signals (t ≳ 2.5) they are mostly **overhead** (lower adoption, little FDR gain). **门槛 ≠ 提高收益** — a precision device, not a return engine.

**Necessary condition:** Evaluate criteria on **excess** returns (information ratio), not absolute. Under absolute returns the market component inflates volatility and can **silently reject even a true t=4** (paper Table 7: DSR reject rate = 1.00 at every strength before the excess correction).

**Lab / paper numbers:**

```
compression_foil: absolute_SR≈1.594  excess_IR≈5.276  ratio≈3.31×
Table 7 (absolute, pre-correction) t≈4: G6=0.40 F6=0.20 F1=0.80 DSR=1.00
after excess @ t=4: hit≈98.3% FDR≈0
```

**MinervaScore NAME-ONLY:** [arXiv:2608.23808](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23808) — auditable validation layer; authors’ own sealed forward null (Spearman ρ≈0.013) — **NOT Seal-as-alpha**. Pointer also in `factor-workflow`.

## Common mistakes / pitfalls

1. Same-bar fills labeled “research.”
2. Reporting the best of many trials without N.
3. Interpreting equal honest NAV tricks as anti-look-ahead proof.
4. Ignoring survivorship while “fixing” on today’s index members.
5. Treating PSR/DSR thresholds as live trading rules without reading the papers’ assumptions (IID / stationary ergodic returns, independent trials).
6. Counting CPCV paths as independent DSR trials \(N\).
7. Treating the teaching CPCV sketch as production AFML code.
8. Trusting a chronological split while TEMP_CENTER / EXEC_OPEN still peek.
9. Treating DSR/PBO as proof the information set is clean (oracle foil).
10. Shipping CSCV teaching sketches as production AFML/Bailey code.
11. Forgetting to log every agent-scored candidate when applying DSR(N).
12. Running admission gates on absolute returns (silent reject of true signals).
13. Treating gates / Minerva Seal as proof of higher expected returns.
14. Building a historical universe from today's index members, or using a reconstitution list before its announcement date; hard-coding "Russell rebalances every June" for 2026 onward; applying one Nasdaq-100 rule set (for example the 10% minimum float) to dates on both sides of 2026-05-01.
15. Filling a signal on an FOMC statement (released 14:00 ET) at that day's open; dating a minutes-based feature to the meeting instead of the release.
16. Assuming fills at the IPO offering price; computing a 60-day signal on a stock listed fewer than 60 trading days ago; assuming every lock-up ends exactly 180 days after the IPO.
17. Screening past S&P 500 candidates with today's $22.7 billion threshold, or treating "eligible" as "will be added"; trading an S&P change before its 05:15 PM ET announcement; filling an after-close earnings release at that day's close or an intraday release at that day's open; dating earnings to the quarter end.
